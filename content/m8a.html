Product management is one of the routes to senior, decision-making work that your background supports well. It combines everything in this course: customers, economics, operations and influence.

The role

A product manager is responsible for a product being valuable to customers and viable for the business. The job is to decide what should be built and why. Engineering decides how. Design decides how it is experienced. The product manager has no authority over either and must lead through evidence and persuasion, which is Month 7 in daily practice.

Four risks

Marty Cagan frames the work as reducing four risks before much is spent on building.

  • Value: will customers buy it or choose to use it?
  • Usability: can they work out how to use it?
  • Feasibility: can we build it with the time, skills and technology we have?
  • Viability: does it work for our business: sales, support, legal, finance, strategy?

Most failed products were feasible and usable and had no real demand. Value risk deserves the earliest attention.

Discovery and delivery

Discovery finds out what is worth building, quickly and cheaply, with prototypes and customer contact. Delivery builds it to production quality. Organisations that skip discovery run "feature factories": a roadmap of requests is built in order, and nobody checks whether any of it changed customer behaviour.

Outputs and outcomes

An output is a feature shipped. An outcome is a change in customer or business results: time to release an engineering change cut by half, renewal rate up three points. Good product teams are given an outcome to achieve and the freedom to find the solution. Poor ones are handed a list of outputs.

Business-to-business product management

Enterprise and industrial products add complications.

  • The buyer is not the user. Both must be satisfied.
  • A few large customers can dominate requests. Building for one produces a custom project, not a product.
  • Sales and services are part of the whole product, as Month 4 described.
  • Long cycles. Feedback on whether something worked can take a year, so leading indicators such as adoption matter.
  • Deep domain knowledge counts for much more than in consumer products. This is where a manufacturing consultant has an edge over a generalist.

Related roles

Product owner usually means managing a development team's backlog. Product marketing owns positioning and launch. Portfolio management allocates investment across products. Titles vary by company, so read the responsibilities.

From outcome to solution

Teresa Torres's opportunity solution tree gives discovery a structure.

  1. Outcome: the measurable result the team is asked to achieve.
  2. Opportunities: customer needs, pains and desires that, if addressed, would move the outcome. These come from interviews.
  3. Solutions: several ideas for each chosen opportunity.
  4. Assumption tests: small experiments on what must be true for a solution to work.

The tree keeps a team from falling in love with its first idea, and makes it clear which customer need each feature is meant to serve.

Talking to customers

The Month 4 rules apply: ask about specific past behaviour, not opinions about the future. Good product teams talk to customers every week, in small doses, and keep doing it after launch.

Minimum viable product

Eric Ries defines it as the version that allows the most learning about customers for the least effort. It is a tool for learning. It is often misread as "the first release with fewer features", shipped without any plan to learn from it.

Build, measure, learn

The lean start-up loop: state a hypothesis, build the smallest thing that tests it, measure what customers actually do, and decide whether to persevere or change direction. A change of direction based on evidence is a pivot. The discipline lies in stating beforehand what result would count as failure.

Evidence, strongest to weakest

  1. Customers pay or commit resources.
  2. Customers use it repeatedly without being pushed.
  3. Customers take a costly action, such as giving data or time.
  4. Customers say they would use it.
  5. Internal opinion.

Many roadmaps are built on levels four and five.

There are always more ideas than capacity. Prioritising is where product strategy becomes real, and where a product manager spends most of their credibility.

Product strategy first

Prioritisation without a strategy is ranking by whoever shouts loudest. A product strategy applies Rumelt's kernel to one product: which customers and problems come first over the next year or two, what the product must become to win them, and what will be left for later. Each candidate feature is judged against it.

RICE

A common scoring method that makes assumptions visible.

RICE score = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort

  • Reach: how many customers or users are affected in a period.
  • Impact: how much each is affected, on a simple scale such as 0.5, 1, 2, 3.
  • Confidence: how sure you are of the first two, as a percentage.
  • Effort: person-months.

A feature reaching 500 customers with impact 2, confidence 80% and effort of 4 person-months scores 500 × 2 × 0.8 ÷ 4 = 200.

The value lies in the conversation it forces: why do we think reach is 500, and how sure are we? The score itself has little precision and should never be treated as the decision. It also ignores strategic fit, dependencies and risk, which must be added by judgement.

Other lenses

  • Kano model. Some features are basics that customers expect and notice only when missing. Some give satisfaction in proportion to how well they are done. Some are delighters nobody asked for. Over time, delighters become basics.
  • Cost of delay. What does each month of waiting cost? Short, valuable items go first.
  • Technical debt and platform work need a reserved share of capacity, since they never win a feature-by-feature contest and their neglect slows everything.

Roadmaps

A roadmap states intent. A list of features with dates stretching two years ahead is a set of promises made with the least information. A sturdier format is now, next, later, organised by customer problems or outcomes, with precision falling as the horizon lengthens. Enterprise customers do need some dated commitments, so make few, make them carefully and keep them.

Metrics

  • A north-star metric captures the value customers get, such as the number of engineering changes released through the product each month.
  • Adoption: share of licensed users active, and breadth of features used.
  • Retention and expansion: the measures from Month 4.
  • Time to value: how long from purchase to first real use.
  • Customer satisfaction, with care: surveys such as net promoter score are easy to collect and weakly linked to behaviour.

Choose a few measures that reflect customer value, and beware of ones that can rise while customers are no better off.

Saying no

Most of the job is declining good ideas. Do it with reasons tied to strategy and evidence, offer what you can, and be consistent. People accept a no they understand, as the fairness research in Month 7 showed.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the RICE score for each.
  2. What would a ranking by score alone choose within 11 person-months?
  3. What does the score leave out for A, C and D?
  4. How would you check the claim that D is a condition of renewal?

Model answer

1. A: 400 × 2 × 0.5 ÷ 8 = 50. B: 800 × 1 × 0.9 ÷ 3 = 240. C: 150 × 3 × 0.8 ÷ 6 = 60. D: 5 × 3 × 1 ÷ 2 = 7.5.

2. B, then C, using 9 person-months, with D fitting into the remaining 2. A, the most strategic item, is left out.

3. A: strategic fit. It is the stated direction and a point of difference, and its confidence is low because nobody has tested it. That calls for discovery, not for dropping it. C: competitive necessity. An entrant already has it, it removes three months from every implementation, which shortens time to value and frees services capacity, and it is a point of parity that will soon be expected. D: revenue at risk. Eight percent of revenue outweighs a reach of five in any sensible reading, if the threat is real.

4. Speak to the customer directly, with the account executive: what regulation requires the report, by when, and what they do today. Find out whether a configurable report or a services-built one would meet the need, and whether other customers in the same industry face the same rule, in which case it is a product feature with wider reach. Sales people under renewal pressure describe wishes as conditions, and a ten-minute call usually settles it.

5. A defensible plan: build C in full (6), since it is necessary, valuable and well evidenced. Build B (3), which is cheap and reaches most users. That uses 9. Put the remaining 2 into a discovery sprint on A: prototype with five customers to find out whether predictions are trusted and acted on, so that next quarter's decision rests on evidence. Handle D through services as a configured report, after confirming the requirement, and generalise it later if other regulated customers need it. Explain the decision with the reasoning and the evidence, tell the AI sponsors exactly what will be learned this quarter and when the build decision comes, and tell the account executive what the customer will receive and by when.

What the case teaches. A scoring model organises the facts and exposes weak assumptions. Strategy, competition and risk then adjust the order. Low confidence on a strategic item is a reason to buy information cheaply, which echoes the pilot case in Month 2.

Digital thread and digital twin

A digital thread is the connected flow of product and process data across design, manufacturing, supply chain and service. Its economic value is in what it removes: re-keying, mismatched revisions, late discovery of problems, and the time people spend finding and reconciling data. A digital twin is a model of a product, process or plant, kept in step with reality, that can be used to predict and decide. Its value is in decisions made before money is spent on physical change.

Why pilots do not scale

The pattern is well documented, including in the World Economic Forum's work on leading factories: many manufacturers run successful pilots and few scale them across plants. The reasons combine everything in this course.

  • No business owner. Pilots run by innovation or IT teams have no line manager accountable for results.
  • No baseline. Benefits cannot be proved, so funding stops.
  • Selection. The pilot plant was the willing, well-run one (Month 2).
  • Foundations missing. Master data, connectivity and standards differ at each plant.
  • Change not managed. The tool arrives and the way of working stays (Month 7).
  • Use cases chosen for novelty and not for value at the constraint (Month 5).
  • Economics do not hold at scale once integration and support costs are counted.

What firms that scale do differently

  • Choose a handful of use cases by value, and design them for scale from the start.
  • Give each a line owner and a financial target that appears in the budget.
  • Invest in shared foundations once: data model, platform, skills.
  • Build internal capability, and avoid depending on vendors indefinitely.
  • Roll out in waves, with each plant's team trained by the last.

Operating models built on software and data

Iansiti and Lakhani argue that firms whose core processes run on software and data scale differently: the marginal cost of serving another customer is low, and the system improves with use. Traditional manufacturers cannot become such firms wholesale, and they can move specific processes, such as planning or quality control, onto that footing.

AI as cheap prediction

Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb describe machine learning as a sharp fall in the cost of prediction: using information you have to produce information you lack. Will this bearing fail within a week? Is this weld defective? What will demand be next month?

Month 2's economics then applies. When something becomes cheap, people use more of it, and its complements become more valuable. The complements of prediction are:

  • Data to train and run on.
  • Judgement: deciding what matters and what a wrong prediction costs.
  • Action: the ability to do something with the prediction.

A decision has parts: data, prediction, judgement, action and outcome. AI lowers the cost of one of them. The others still need people, process and authority.

Judging a use case

  1. What decision would change, and how often is it made?
  2. What is a better prediction worth each time?
  3. What does each kind of error cost? Recall the base-rate problem from Month 2: a rare event and a modest false-alarm rate mean most alerts are false.
  4. Is there data of sufficient quantity and quality, with outcomes recorded?
  5. Can anyone act on the prediction in time?
  6. Who is accountable when it is wrong?

Generative AI

Large language models generate text, code, images and structured output from instructions. They are good at drafting, summarising, translating between formats, answering questions over documents, writing and explaining code, and working through multi-step tasks with tools.

Their limits matter for managers.

  • They can state falsehoods fluently and confidently. Output that matters must be checked.
  • They reflect their training data and know nothing of your company unless given it.
  • Performance is uneven: excellent at some hard tasks and poor at some easy ones. Ethan Mollick calls this a jagged frontier, and it has to be found by trial.
  • Results vary with how the task is framed.
  • Confidential data and intellectual property need deliberate handling.

The field changes quickly. Treat any specific claim about capability, including this one, as dated and test current tools yourself.

Agents

An AI agent is given a goal and tools and carries out a series of steps with some autonomy. The management questions are those of delegation in Month 7: what may it do alone, what needs approval, how is its work checked, who answers for the result.

Effects on work

Early studies of knowledge work with AI assistance generally find faster output and often better quality on tasks within the tools' competence, with the largest gains for less experienced people, and worse results when people rely on the tools outside it. Tasks are automated before jobs are. Jobs are rearranged around what remains: defining the problem, judgement, checking, relationships and accountability.

Build, buy or adapt

  • Buy capabilities embedded in software you already use, for common tasks.
  • Adapt general models with your own data and context for tasks specific to your business.
  • Build your own models only where you have unique data and the use is central to your advantage.

The firms that get value from AI at scale differ from the rest mainly in data, operating model and governance. The algorithms are available to everyone.

Data as a foundation

  • Quality: accurate, complete, consistent, current. Most industrial AI projects spend most of their effort here.
  • Context: sensor readings mean little without knowing the product, process step, machine state and revision. That context sits in engineering and manufacturing systems.
  • Ownership: someone in the business accountable for each important data set.
  • Access: the people and systems that need it can get it, under control.

A manufacturer whose bills of material, routings and change records are inconsistent across plants cannot scale AI planning or quality models, whatever it buys. Data foundations are unglamorous and they decide the outcome.

Choosing use cases

Score candidates on value (size, and whether it is at a constraint), feasibility (data readiness, technical difficulty, integration) and adoption (owner, change required, willingness). Choose a few high-value, feasible ones with committed owners. Use early results to fund the foundations that later ones need.

Operating model

  • A small central team for platform, standards and scarce skills.
  • Business units owning the use cases and their benefits.
  • Joint teams of domain experts, data specialists and IT.
  • Training for managers and front-line staff, since understanding among users is the main limit on adoption.

Measuring value

Set a baseline before deployment. Use a control group where possible, as in Month 2. Count full costs, including integration, running, monitoring and change. Put the benefit into the budget of the owning unit. A benefit nobody is accountable for will not appear.

Model answer

1. Value per rupee: pilot 5 at 3.5, pilots 1 and 4 at 2.0, pilot 3 at 1.9, pilot 2 at 1.8.

2. Pilots 5, 1, 4 and 2 cost 2 + 6 + 1 + 5 = ₹14 crore and offer 7 + 12 + 2 + 9 = ₹30 crore a year. That fits the budget with ₹1 crore to spare.

3. Its data are not ready: routings differ by plant, so a scheduling model built at one plant will not run at another without first standardising master data. It has no owner in the line. Scheduling changes how planners and supervisors work every day, so it needs the most change management. Scaling it now would repeat the pattern that produced fourteen stalled pilots.

4. Treat them with caution. They are extrapolated from a single, probably willing, plant by the team that ran the pilots. The predictive maintenance value depends on presses being the constraint: at the five plants where they are, each hour saved is extra contribution, and at the other three it is only repair cost avoided, so the true figure may be closer to two thirds of 12. To firm up: set baselines at each plant before roll-out, have finance and the plant heads agree the benefit calculation, and take a conservative figure into budgets.

5. Ownership: each use case belongs to a line executive. Plant heads own 1 and 5, the quality head owns 2, the maintenance head owns 4. The benefits go into their budgets and scorecards. The digital team becomes a delivery partner. Sequence: start with energy optimisation, which is cheap, fast and visible, and gives the short-term win. Run predictive maintenance first at the five constrained plants, in two waves, with each wave's team training the next. Follow with vision inspection after a data clean-up. Roll out the manuals assistant alongside, since it is low risk and builds familiarity with AI among technicians. Measurement: baseline now, monthly reporting by the owners, and a review after six months that decides the following year's funding.

6. Use the spare ₹1 crore and part of next year's benefits to standardise routings and bills of process across plants, which is engineering and manufacturing master-data work. Appoint the head of planning as owner. Bring scheduling back in a year, when the foundation exists and the organisation has learned to scale four simpler cases.

What the case teaches. Selecting use cases is capital budgeting with two extra tests: are the data ready, and is there an owner? The highest-value item is often the one to postpone. The roll-out design matters more than the choice of algorithms, and it draws on every month of this course.

Five questions, then a one-page roadmap.

A digital initiative should first be able to state:

  1. Which vendor it uses
  2. Which line of the financial statements it moves and by how much
  3. How new the technology is
  4. How many pilots are planned

Value has to land somewhere in the accounts.

A use case has high value, poor data and no line owner. It should be:

  1. Scaled first because of its value
  2. Abandoned permanently
  3. Given to the vendor to run
  4. Postponed while its foundations and ownership are put in place

Feasibility and adoption gate the value.

In the "prediction machines" view, AI reduces the cost of:

  1. Prediction
  2. Judgement
  3. Accountability
  4. Action

The other parts of a decision become more valuable.

A predictive maintenance model flags failures at a plant where the machine concerned is not the constraint. Its benefit is mainly:

  1. Extra contribution on more output
  2. Nothing
  3. Repair cost and disruption avoided
  4. Higher prices

Extra uptime at a non-constraint does not raise plant output.

The strongest protection against an AI system causing physical damage is:

  1. A confident vendor
  2. Hard limits on what it may change, with autonomy widened in stages
  3. A larger model
  4. More training data alone
