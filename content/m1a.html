Every management decision you will ever defend ends up as a number in one of three reports. This first session gives you the map. The next two weeks fill it in.

What a business is, in one sentence

A business raises money, uses it to buy things that produce goods or services, sells those for more than they cost, and returns the surplus to whoever provided the money. Accounting is the scorekeeping system for that loop. Engineers already think this way about a production line: inputs, transformation, outputs, losses. Accounting does the same for money.

Four words people mix up

  • Revenue (also sales, turnover, top line): the value of what you sold in a period, whether or not the customer has paid yet.
  • Cost / expense: the value of resources used up to earn that revenue.
  • Profit (earnings, bottom line): revenue minus expenses. It is an opinion built on accounting rules.
  • Cash: money actually in the bank. It is a fact.

A company can be profitable and still run out of cash, and that is how most healthy-looking firms die. You will see exactly how in Week 2.

Reading it layer by layer

Gross profit is what is left after the direct cost of making the product. A 30% gross margin means every ₹100 of fixtures costs ₹70 to build. This line belongs to operations and purchasing, and it is where most manufacturing improvement projects land.

EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It approximates the cash the operations generate before financing and investment choices. Investors use it to compare companies with different debt levels and asset ages.

EBIT, or operating profit, subtracts depreciation, the yearly charge for wearing out machines and buildings. It measures how good the business is at its core activity, regardless of how it is financed.

Profit after tax is what belongs to the owners once lenders and the government are paid.

Why percentages matter more than rupees

Dividing every line by revenue gives a "common-size" statement. It lets you compare a ₹120 crore firm with a ₹12,000 crore one, and it lets you spot change. If Kaveri's gross margin drops from 30% to 28%, that is ₹2.4 crore of profit gone on the same sales, which is over a quarter of its net profit.

Worked example: a one-point saving

Suppose a line-balancing study cuts cost of goods sold by one percentage point of revenue, from 70% to 69%. Gross profit rises by ₹1.2 crore. Nothing else changes, so profit before tax rises from ₹12.0 to ₹13.2 crore. After 25% tax, net profit rises from ₹9.0 to ₹9.9 crore. A 1.4% cut in COGS produced a 10% rise in net profit. Small cost changes have large profit effects because profit is a thin slice at the bottom.

In your job

Translate technical benefits into the line they hit. Cycle-time reduction and scrap reduction improve gross margin. Faster engineering change handling reduces SG&A or R&D cost. Avoided rework on a new line reduces capital spending, which shows up later as lower depreciation.

The balance sheet lists everything a company controls and shows who paid for it. It always balances, and the reason it balances is the most useful idea in accounting.

The accounting equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Assets are what the company owns or controls. They were paid for with either other people's money (liabilities) or the owners' money (equity). Every rupee of assets has exactly one of those two sources, so the two sides must be equal.

What each line means

  • Inventory: raw material, work in progress and finished goods, valued at cost.
  • Receivables: invoices sent to customers and not yet paid.

  • Loans: money owed to banks, on which interest is paid.

  • Retained earnings: all past profits kept in the business and not paid out as dividends.

How the statements connect

Retained earnings rose from ₹29 crore to ₹38 crore. The difference is ₹9 crore, which is exactly the FY26 profit after tax, because Kaveri paid no dividend. This is the first link between statements: profit flows into equity.

Now look at what changed on the asset side. Receivables grew by ₹8 crore and inventory by ₹4 crore. Kaveri sold more and built more, but ₹12 crore of that growth is sitting in unpaid invoices and stock. That is where the cash went.

Current versus non-current

Items expected to turn into cash, or be paid, within a year are "current": inventory, receivables, cash, payables, short-term loans. The rest are non-current. Current assets of ₹52 crore against current liabilities of ₹24 crore means Kaveri can cover its near-term bills about twice over.

In your job

Inventory and fixed assets are the two balance-sheet lines digital manufacturing touches. A better plant layout or a validated process plan means less work-in-progress and fewer machines for the same output. Call it "releasing capital" in front of a CFO.

The situation

Kaveri's largest customer, a tier-1 seating supplier, accounts for a third of its sales. In the annual contract review the customer's purchasing head asks for a 5% price reduction across all fixtures, effective next year. This is routine in automotive supply chains, where buyers expect suppliers to pass on yearly productivity gains.

Kaveri's managing director, Meera Kulkarni, calls her team. The sales head says the customer will move volume to a competitor if Kaveri refuses. The plant head says there is no fat left to cut. The finance head puts last year's income statement on the screen (the one from session 2) and says, "Before we argue, let us see what 5% actually does."

To keep the arithmetic clean, assume the 5% reduction ends up applying to all of Kaveri's revenue, with volumes and all costs unchanged.

Your questions

  1. Recalculate revenue, EBIT, profit before tax and profit after tax under the price cut. By what percentage does each fall?
  2. How much would cost of goods sold have to fall, in ₹ crore and as a percentage, to fully restore EBIT?
  3. Meera has three options: refuse, accept, or accept in exchange for something. What would you ask for in return, and why?
  4. A software vendor offers Kaveri a simulation and process-planning package for ₹1.5 crore that promises a 3% reduction in cost of goods sold. On the numbers alone, is that interesting?
Model answer

1. Revenue falls 5% from ₹120 crore to ₹114 crore, a loss of ₹6 crore. Costs do not change, so the full ₹6 crore falls through every profit line. EBIT drops from 15 to 9 (down 40%). Profit before tax drops from 12 to 6 (down 50%). Tax at 25% is 1.5, so profit after tax drops from 9.0 to 4.5 (down 50%). A 5% price change halves net profit. Price is the most powerful profit lever in any business.

2. COGS must fall by ₹6 crore, from 84 to 78. That is a 7.1% reduction in cost to offset a 5% reduction in price, because cost is a smaller base than revenue.

3. Good answers trade price for something that lowers Kaveri's cost or risk: a longer contract or volume commitment (spreads fixed cost), faster payment terms (frees cash, as Week 2 shows), design standardisation across the customer's fixtures (cuts engineering hours), or phasing the reduction over two years while productivity projects take effect. Refusing outright risks a third of sales. Accepting with nothing in return halves profit.

4. A 3% cut in COGS is 0.03 × 84 = ₹2.5 crore a year, against a ₹1.5 crore cost. That pays back in about seven months if the saving is real. The right response is to test the claim: which cost lines fall, by how much, and how soon. You will build that kind of business case properly in Week 4.

What the case teaches. Profit is a thin residual, so percentage changes in price or cost are magnified at the bottom line. This magnification is called operating leverage. It is also why purchasing departments push so hard on price, and why suppliers who can show verified cost reductions have real bargaining power.

Percent and ratio questions make up a large share of Quant and most of the arithmetic inside Data Insights. They are also the arithmetic of every business case.

Rules worth memorising

  • Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. The base is always the old value. Going from 80 to 100 is +25%. Going from 100 to 80 is −20%.
  • Multipliers: a 15% rise is × 1.15, a 15% fall is × 0.85. Chain them for successive changes: up 10% then up 20% is × 1.1 × 1.2 = × 1.32.
  • Percentage points are differences between percentages. A margin moving from 30% to 28% fell by 2 percentage points, which is a 6.7% fall.
  • Markup and margin: markup is profit ÷ cost, margin is profit ÷ price. A 25% markup is a 20% margin.
  • Ratios: a ratio of 5:3 means 5 parts and 3 parts out of 8. Find the size of one part first. To join A:B and B:C, make the B terms equal.

Worked example

A component's price rises 25%. A buyer wants to spend the same total. Spending = price × quantity. Price becomes 1.25 times, so quantity must become 1 ÷ 1.25 = 0.8 times. The buyer cuts quantity by 20%, not 25%.

Accounting records events when they happen economically, not when money moves. This one rule explains most of the difference between profit and cash, and it decides how software deals are reported.

The accrual principle

Revenue is recorded when it is earned. Expenses are recorded in the period they help earn revenue, which is called matching. Cash can move earlier or later.

Revenue recognition for software

Under Ind AS 115 in India (IFRS 15 internationally), revenue is recognised as the seller fulfils its promises to the customer. For software this matters a great deal.

Subscription. A customer signs a three-year subscription at ₹1.2 crore a year, billed each 1 January in advance. The vendor's year ends 31 March. By 31 March it has delivered three months of service, so it recognises ₹0.3 crore of revenue. The other ₹0.9 crore is deferred revenue, a liability, because the vendor still owes nine months of service. Cash received: ₹1.2 crore. Revenue: ₹0.3 crore.

Perpetual licence with maintenance. A ₹3 crore perpetual licence is typically recognised when the customer can use the software, with annual maintenance recognised over each year. Revenue arrives early and in lumps.

This is why a vendor moving from perpetual licences to subscriptions often shows falling revenue for a while even when it is selling more. The contract value has not shrunk; it is being recognised over a longer time.

Long projects

For a six-month implementation or a custom robotic cell, revenue is usually recognised over time in proportion to work completed, often measured by cost incurred against total expected cost. If a ₹10 crore project has used ₹3 crore of an expected ₹7.5 crore cost, it is 40% complete and ₹4 crore of revenue is recognised.

In your job

Deal structure changes what your employer and your customer report. A subscription spreads the customer's expense and the vendor's revenue. Milestone-based services contracts move revenue with delivery progress. When sales leaders care about quarter-end, this is the reason.

The cash flow statement reconciles profit to the change in the bank balance. It has three parts, and each part answers a different question about the company.

Three kinds of cash flow

  • Operating (CFO): cash generated by the day-to-day business.
  • Investing (CFI): cash spent on, or received from, long-term assets. Buying machines is capital expenditure, or capex.
  • Financing (CFF): cash from or to lenders and owners: loans taken or repaid, shares issued, dividends paid.

How to read the adjustments

Depreciation is added back because it reduced profit without any cash leaving. The cash left when the machine was bought.

An increase in an asset uses cash. More inventory means cash was spent on stock. More receivables means sales were booked but not collected.

An increase in a liability provides cash. Higher payables mean Kaveri has delayed paying suppliers.

The capex figure also ties to the balance sheet: opening fixed assets of 37, plus capex of 8, minus depreciation of 5, gives the closing 40.

The story in these numbers

Kaveri earned ₹9 crore, but only ₹4 crore of it turned into operating cash because ₹10 crore net went into inventory and unpaid invoices. It then spent ₹8 crore on new equipment. To cover the gap it borrowed ₹4 crore short term and still drew down ₹2 crore of cash. The business is growing and profitable, and it is becoming more dependent on its bank every year.

A useful single number is free cash flow: cash from operations minus capex. For Kaveri that is 4 − 8 = −₹4 crore. Negative free cash flow in a growing company is common. It becomes dangerous when lenders stop funding it.

Critical reasoning questions give you a short argument and ask you to weaken it, strengthen it, find its assumption or draw a conclusion. The same skill lets you take apart a weak business case in a meeting.

Find the parts first

  • Conclusion: what the author wants you to believe. Look for "therefore", "so", "concludes", or a recommendation.
  • Premises: the evidence offered.
  • Assumption: an unstated claim that must be true for the premises to lead to the conclusion.

Nearly every question is about the gap between premises and conclusion. State that gap in your own words before you read the options.

Three gaps that appear constantly

  1. Correlation treated as cause. Two things happened together, so one caused the other. Attack it with another cause, reversed cause, or coincidence.
  2. Unrepresentative sample. The group studied differs from the group the conclusion is about, often because people chose to be in it.
  3. Costs ignored. A plan has a benefit, so it should be adopted. Attack it with an offsetting cost or side effect.

Method

Read the question stem first so you know your task. Read the argument and mark the conclusion. Predict the gap. Eliminate options that are off-topic or that affect the premises but not the link to the conclusion. To test an assumption, negate it: if the argument collapses, it is a necessary assumption.

Practice

A sales team that used a new demo environment closed 30% more deals than a team that did not. Management concludes that giving the environment to every team will raise deals closed. The argument assumes that:

  1. The demo environment is cheap to roll out
  2. All customers prefer demos to presentations
  3. The team using it was not already stronger than the other team for other reasons
  4. Deals closed is the only measure of sales performance

If the first team was simply better, the difference is not due to the tool. Negate (c) and the argument falls apart.

Working capital is the money trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. It behaves like work-in-progress on a line: invisible when things flow, expensive when they do not.

Definitions

Net working capital = Inventory + Receivables − Payables

For Kaveri: 18 + 30 − 14 = ₹34 crore. That much of its capital does nothing except wait.

Measuring it in days

MeasureFormulaKaveri FY26
Days sales outstanding (DSO)Receivables ÷ Revenue × 36530 ÷ 120 × 365 = 91 days
Days inventory outstanding (DIO)Inventory ÷ COGS × 36518 ÷ 84 × 365 = 78 days
Days payables outstanding (DPO)Payables ÷ COGS × 36514 ÷ 84 × 365 = 61 days
Cash conversion cycleDSO + DIO − DPO91 + 78 − 61 = 108 days

Inventory and payables are divided by cost of goods sold because both are measured at cost. Receivables are divided by revenue because they are measured at selling price.

The cash conversion cycle says that from the day Kaveri pays for steel to the day its customer pays for the finished fixture, 108 days pass. Kaveri must fund every one of them.

Why growth eats cash

If sales grow 25% and the cycle stays at 108 days, working capital grows about 25% too. For Kaveri that is roughly ₹8.5 crore of extra cash needed, close to a full year's profit. Fast-growing profitable firms go bankrupt this way. It is called overtrading.

What each day is worth

One day of receivables is 120 ÷ 365 = ₹0.33 crore. Collecting ten days faster frees ₹3.3 crore once, permanently. One day of inventory is 84 ÷ 365 = ₹0.23 crore. Cutting inventory by 15 days frees ₹3.5 crore. At a 10% borrowing rate that also saves about ₹0.35 crore of interest every year.

The levers

  • Inventory: shorter lead times, smaller batches, fewer engineering changes arriving late, better planning accuracy.
  • Receivables: milestone billing, advances, fewer disputes at acceptance, right-first-time commissioning.
  • Payables: longer supplier terms, used with care because suppliers have the same problem.

In your job

Lead-time reduction is a cash argument as well as a cost argument. If virtual commissioning shortens installation and customer acceptance by three weeks, a machine builder invoices and collects three weeks sooner on every project. Express that as days of receivables and work-in-progress released.

A profitable supplier wins its biggest order and nearly fails. Work out why before you open the model answer.

The situation

Trimurti Precision is a fictional tier-2 machining company near Chakan. Last year it had revenue of ₹40 crore. This year it won a large programme from an electric two-wheeler maker and revenue jumped 50% to ₹60 crore. Net profit margin held at 6%, and depreciation was ₹1.5 crore.

The founder, Sandeep Jagtap, expected a comfortable year. In January his finance manager told him the company could not pay salaries on time without a new loan.

The facts: customers pay in 90 days on average. Inventory covers 60 days. Suppliers are paid in 45 days. Trimurti also bought two machining centres for ₹4 crore to handle the new programme. For a quick estimate, treat net working capital as revenue × cash conversion cycle ÷ 365.

Your questions

  1. What is Trimurti's cash conversion cycle?
  2. Estimate net working capital at ₹40 crore and at ₹60 crore of revenue. How much extra cash did growth absorb?
  3. Estimate cash from operations and free cash flow for the year. How much external funding did Trimurti need?
  4. Sandeep can ask the customer for something, change something internally, or go to the bank. Give one action of each kind and say which you would do first.
Model answer

1. 90 + 60 − 45 = 105 days.

2. At ₹40 crore: 40 × 105 ÷ 365 = ₹11.5 crore. At ₹60 crore: 60 × 105 ÷ 365 = ₹17.3 crore. Growth absorbed about ₹5.8 crore. This estimate is rough, because inventory and payables should strictly be based on cost, but it is close enough to see the problem.

3. Net profit is 6% of 60 = ₹3.6 crore. Add depreciation of 1.5 to get 5.1. Subtract the working capital increase of 5.8. Cash from operations is about −₹0.7 crore. After capex of ₹4 crore, free cash flow is about −₹4.7 crore. Trimurti needed nearly ₹5 crore from outside in its most profitable year.

4. Customer: negotiate 60-day terms or an advance for tooling, since the customer needs the supplier to survive the ramp-up. Thirty days less on ₹60 crore of sales frees about ₹4.9 crore. Internal: cut inventory days through smaller batches and tighter scheduling; fifteen days frees roughly ₹2 to 2.5 crore. Bank: a working capital limit secured on receivables, or invoice discounting. The bank is fastest and should be arranged first to avoid a missed payroll, but it treats the symptom. The customer terms conversation fixes the cause and should start the same week.

What the case teaches. Growth needs funding in proportion to the cash conversion cycle. Before accepting a large order, a manager should ask how many days of working capital it needs and who will finance them.

Most Quant word problems are a translation exercise. Turn the sentences into equations and the algebra is usually short.

Translation rules

  • "Is", "was", "costs" become =.
  • "Of" with a fraction or percent means multiply.
  • "More than" and "less than" reverse order: "5 less than x" is x − 5.
  • Name the unknown the question asks for, so you finish on the answer.

Two equations, two unknowns

Use substitution or elimination. Example: two robots and three fixtures cost ₹95 lakh. One robot and one fixture cost ₹40 lakh. From the second, r = 40 − f. Substitute: 2(40 − f) + 3f = 95, so 80 + f = 95, giving f = 15 and r = 25.

Inequalities

Treat them like equations, with one extra rule: multiplying or dividing by a negative number flips the sign.

Mixtures

Track the amount of the pure ingredient. Amount = concentration × total.
