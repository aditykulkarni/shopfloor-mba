You negotiate constantly: scope, deadlines, resources, salary. Most people do it by instinct and leave value behind. A small amount of structure changes results.

Four terms

  • BATNA: your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. What you will do if this deal fails.
  • Reservation value: the worst deal you would accept, set by your BATNA.
  • ZOPA: the zone of possible agreement, between the two sides' reservation values.
  • Target: the outcome you are aiming for, set ambitiously within what you can justify.

A seller will not go below ₹80 lakh. A buyer will not go above ₹100 lakh. The zone runs from 80 to 100, and any price in it leaves both better off than walking away. Negotiation decides where in the zone the deal lands. If the seller's floor were 105, there is no zone and no deal should be made.

Your BATNA is your power

The party with the better alternative has the stronger position, whatever their size or manner. The most productive preparation is to improve your alternative before the talks: a second supplier, another job offer, a different customer. The second most productive is to understand theirs. What will they really do if this fails?

Anchoring

The first number put on the table pulls the outcome towards it, as the biases session showed. Guidance from the research:

  • If you know the range well, make the first offer, and make it ambitious but defensible.
  • If you know little, let them go first, and do not let their number anchor you. Respond with your own figure based on your own analysis.
  • Always attach a reason to a number. Reasons make anchors stick.

Concessions

  • Plan them in advance.
  • Make them smaller each time, which signals you are near your limit.
  • Never give one without asking for something. This is the give-and-get rule from Month 4.
  • Label a concession as one, so that it is counted.

Preparation checklist

  1. My interests: what do I actually need, and why?
  2. My BATNA and reservation value.
  3. Their interests, constraints and likely BATNA.
  4. The issues beyond price.
  5. My target and opening, with reasons.
  6. What I can give that costs me little and helps them, and the reverse.

Twenty minutes on these six points puts you ahead of most counterparts.

  2. ₹52 to 60 lakh
  3. Exactly ₹56 lakh

Any price between the two reservation values works for both.

The most reliable way to strengthen your position before a negotiation is to:

  1. Appear confident
  2. Open aggressively
  3. Improve your alternative to the deal

You know the market well and want to shape the outcome. You should usually:

  1. Make the first offer, ambitious and justified
  2. Wait for their offer
  3. Open at your reservation value

A well-supported first offer anchors the discussion.

Dividing a fixed amount is half of negotiation. The better half is making the amount larger before dividing it.

Positions and interests

A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Two people argue over one orange. Splitting it gives each half. If one wants the juice and the other the peel for baking, each can have all of what they wanted. The example is trivial, and the same structure appears in nearly every commercial negotiation.

Ask "why does that matter to you?" and "what would that let you do?" People reveal interests when asked with real curiosity.

Trading across issues

When there are several issues and the two sides value them differently, each can give way on what matters less to it in exchange for what matters more. This is called logrolling.

IssueWorth to the vendorCost to the customer
Three-year term instead of one+15−5
IssueWorth to the customerCost to the vendor
Quarterly payment instead of annual in advance+8−2

If the customer accepts the three-year term and the vendor accepts quarterly payment, the vendor gains 15 − 2 = 13 and the customer gains 8 − 5 = 3. Together they are 16 better off than under a one-year contract paid annually. A price adjustment can then share that gain more evenly. Arguing over price alone would have created nothing.

How to find trades

  • Put several issues on the table at once. Settling them one at a time turns each into a tug of war.
  • Make package offers. "If we do A and B, we can do C."
  • Offer two packages of equal value to you and ask which they prefer. Their choice shows what they value.
  • Share information about priorities, though not your reservation value.
  • Use differences in forecasts, risk tolerance and timing. A payment that depends on results suits two sides that disagree about the future.

Principled negotiation

Fisher and Ury's four rules: separate the people from the problem; focus on interests; invent options for mutual gain; insist on objective criteria such as market rates or precedent. The last gives both sides a way to move without losing face.

Creating and claiming

Value still has to be divided once it is created, and being open about priorities carries some risk. The practical balance: be generous with information about what matters to you, careful with your limits, and firm on the criteria for dividing the gain.

Relationships

Most business negotiations are repeated games. An agreement that leaves the other side feeling cheated will be paid for in the next round, in delivery or at renewal.

Six principles of persuasion

Robert Cialdini's research identifies tendencies that shape how people respond to requests.

PrincipleTendencyHonest use at work
ReciprocityPeople return favoursHelp others before you need anything
Commitment and consistencyPeople act in line with what they have saidGet a small public agreement first
Social proofPeople follow what similar others doShow who else has done it
AuthorityPeople defer to credible expertsEstablish your and your sources' credentials
LikingPeople agree with those they like and who are like themFind real common ground
ScarcityPeople value what is limitedState real deadlines and constraints

These can be used to manipulate. Used with accurate information and for an outcome the other person would endorse, they are simply good communication. The test is whether you would be comfortable if they saw exactly what you were doing.

Currencies

Cohen and Bradford describe influence as exchange. People cooperate when they get something they value. The currencies are wider than money: information, visibility, recognition, resources, a favour returned, the chance to learn, help with their own priorities. To influence someone, work out what they need and what you have to offer.

Stakeholder mapping

For any initiative, list those affected and place each by power over the outcome and attitude towards it.

  • High power, supportive: keep closely involved and ask them to speak for it.
  • High power, opposed or unsure: understand their concerns first. Their objection can stop you.
  • Low power, supportive: give them a role and visibility.
  • Low power, opposed: keep informed and listen for valid criticism.

Speak to key people individually before the meeting where the decision is taken. A proposal that surprises a senior person in public usually fails, however good it is.

Managing upward

  • Know your manager's goals, pressures and preferred way of receiving information.
  • Bring solutions with options and a recommendation, not only problems.
  • No surprises. Bad news early.
  • Make it easy to say yes: a short written summary, the decision needed, the deadline.
  • Deliver. Reliability is the main source of influence with anyone senior.

Building a network before you need it

Relationships formed when you want nothing are the ones that help when you do. People who connect groups that do not normally talk to each other hold unusual influence, as the power session noted.

The situation

Rohan Shinde is a manufacturing software consultant in Pune with five years of experience. A mid-sized European consultancy has offered him a senior consultant role at its Dutch office: a base salary of €62,000, standard benefits, a start date in eight weeks, and no mention of relocation costs. The hiring manager, Annelies, was enthusiastic in interviews and said the team is short of people with his background.

Rohan's situation: his current employer has hinted at a promotion in six months with no figure attached. He has no other offer. He estimates the move would cost him about €6,000. He would like €70,000, a title of "lead consultant", and time to finish a certification. The recruiter has told him the salary band for the role tops out "around 68". He also knows the firm must sponsor a work permit, which takes time.

Your questions

  1. What is Rohan's BATNA and how strong is it? What is the firm's?
  2. List all the issues that could be on the table.
  3. Which matter more to Rohan than they cost the firm, and the reverse?
  4. Draft the approach: what to ask for, in what order, and how to justify it.
  5. What mistakes should he avoid?
Model answer

1. Rohan's alternative is staying in his current job with a possible promotion. It is acceptable and not strong, since it has no number and no certainty. The firm's alternative is to continue searching for a scarce profile while its team is short-handed, which is costly in lost billable work. Each side has reason to agree, and the firm's need is real. Rohan should not overestimate his leverage, and he should not underestimate it either.

2. Base salary. Level and title. Relocation support. Sign-on payment. Start date. Training and certification budget and time. Bonus terms. Timing of the first salary review. Help with housing search and permit costs. Remote working arrangements. Family-related support.

3. Cheap for the firm and valuable to Rohan: relocation costs as a one-off payment, which does not disturb salary bands; a certification budget, which the firm benefits from; a later start date of a few weeks; a guaranteed salary review at six or twelve months. Valuable to the firm and cheap for Rohan: flexibility on title, an early commitment so they can plan projects, and a willingness to start on a specific client engagement.

4. First, express real enthusiasm and say he wants to make it work, so that the request is read as problem-solving. Second, raise everything together and not one item at a time: "There are three things that would let me accept quickly." Third, ask for a base at the top of the band, about €68,000, justified by scarce experience across two vendor platforms and by what he will bill, plus relocation of €6,000 as a one-off, plus the certification budget and a salary review at twelve months. Fourth, show flexibility on title and start date. Fifth, if the base cannot move beyond, say, €65,000, ask for the difference as a sign-on payment and an earlier review. Throughout, give reasons and ask what constraints Annelies faces.

5. Negotiating salary alone and forgetting the cheaper items. Making demands one after another, which feels like the goalposts keep moving. Inventing a competing offer: it is dishonest and easily exposed. Issuing an ultimatum. Negotiating by email when a call would build trust. Accepting on the spot without asking, since a reasonable request made politely almost never costs an offer. And failing to get the final terms in writing.

What the case teaches. A job offer has many issues with different value to each side, so it is an integrative negotiation. People who treat it as a single number get less and enjoy it less. Likeability and good reasons matter: the other side has to want to help you and be able to justify it internally.

Leadership is taught last because it draws on everything before it. It is a set of practices that can be learned, and it starts well before you have a title.

Managing and leading

John Kotter's distinction is useful. Management copes with complexity: planning, budgeting, organising, staffing, controlling. Leadership copes with change: setting direction, aligning people, motivating. Organisations need both, and most roles need both from the same person. A well-managed team heading the wrong way and an inspired team in chaos are equally common failures.

A manager's output

Andrew Grove's definition: a manager's output is the output of their organisation plus that of neighbouring organisations under their influence. The implication is that a manager should spend time on activities with high leverage, where an hour of theirs improves many hours of others' work: clarifying goals, training, removing obstacles, making timely decisions, one-to-ones. Doing a subordinate's task better than they would is low leverage, however satisfying.

Adapting to the person

Grove's concept of task-relevant maturity, close to the situational leadership model, says the right style depends on how experienced the person is at the specific task.

Experience with this taskEffective style
LowStructured: say what, how and by when. Check often.
MediumTwo-way: explain reasons, support, discuss.
HighAgree objectives and get out of the way. Monitor lightly.

The same person can be at different levels for different tasks. A strong technical consultant who has never run a customer negotiation needs structure for that task. Using one style for everyone is the commonest error: micromanaging experts or abandoning beginners.

Delegation

  • Delegate the outcome and the authority to achieve it, along with the task.
  • Be explicit about constraints and about what decisions they can take alone.
  • Agree checkpoints in advance. Checking is not distrust when it is planned.
  • Accept that their way will differ from yours.
  • You stay accountable for the result.

What followers want

Large surveys of what people look for in leaders they would follow willingly put honesty first, then competence, a sense of direction, and the ability to inspire. Charisma is far down the list. Consistency between what a leader says and does is what trust is built from.

Decisions

A leader has to be clear which decisions they make alone, which after consulting, and which the team makes. Ambiguity here wastes more energy than a wrong decision. For reversible decisions, speed matters more than precision. For irreversible ones, take the time.

Leading before you manage

You can practise now: run a workshop, mentor an intern, coordinate a proposal, lead a retrospective after a lost deal. Promotion to a first management role usually goes to someone already doing parts of the job.

In your job

Take the next intern or junior colleague you work with through the three styles deliberately as they gain experience on a task. Notice when you are tempted to do it yourself. That impulse is the main thing new managers have to unlearn.

In Kotter's terms, budgeting and staffing are part of:

  1. Leadership
  2. Strategy
  3. Management

Management handles complexity. Leadership handles change.

  2. Specify each step

Ethical failures in business seldom come from villains. They come from ordinary people under pressure who take a small step, and then another. Preparation is what protects you.

Three lenses

  • Consequences: which action leads to the best outcome for all affected?
  • Duties and rights: is the action honest and fair, and does it respect others' rights, whatever the outcome?
  • Character: what would a person of integrity do, and what does doing this make me?

When all three agree the answer is clear. When they conflict, you have a real dilemma and should slow down.

Why good people do bad things

  • Incremental steps. Each step is a small extension of the last.
  • Pressure and targets. Goals that cannot be met honestly invite dishonesty.
  • Obedience and conformity. "My manager asked" and "everyone does it".
  • Rationalisation. "It hurts nobody." "We will fix it later." "It is expected in this market."
  • Motivated blindness. People do not notice what it is against their interest to notice.

Practical tests

  • Would I be comfortable if this appeared in the press, or if I had to explain it to my family?
  • Would I accept it if I were the customer?
  • Am I being asked to keep it quiet?
  • Is this the first step of something I would not do in full?

Situations you will meet

  • Overstating a product's present capabilities to win a deal.
  • Pressure to bring revenue forward at quarter-end by promising terms outside the contract.
  • Use of a customer's confidential information on another customer's project.
  • Gifts, hospitality and payments to influence a purchase. Indian anti-corruption law, and laws such as the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the UK Bribery Act that apply to multinational firms wherever they operate, make these serious offences.
  • Conflicts of interest, such as recommending a partner in which you have a stake.
  • Reporting project status as better than it is.

Acting on it

Mary Gentile's work starts from the observation that people usually know what is right and do not know how to say it. Her advice is to rehearse: prepare the words, anticipate the rationalisations you will hear and your replies, find allies, and frame the issue in terms of the organisation's own interests and stated values. "If we show that as available and it slips, we lose the renewal and the reference" lands better than an accusation.

Corporate governance

Governance is the system by which a company is directed and controlled.

  • Shareholders own the company and elect the board.
  • The board of directors appoints and oversees the chief executive, approves strategy and major investments, and is accountable for the accounts. Independent directors are there to check management.
  • Board committees: audit, nomination and remuneration, risk.
  • Auditors give an independent opinion on the financial statements.

The underlying issue is the agency problem: managers run the firm with other people's money and their interests can differ from the owners'. In India, where many listed companies have a controlling promoter family, the main tension is often between controlling and minority shareholders, which is why related-party transactions get such attention, as Month 1 noted.

A long-running debate concerns whose interests a company should serve: shareholders alone, or a wider group of employees, customers, suppliers and society. In practice, a firm that damages those groups rarely serves its shareholders for long.

Senior people decide on the basis of short documents and short conversations. Being right is not enough if they cannot see your point in the first minute.

Answer first

Engineers are trained to present background, method, analysis and then the conclusion. Executives want the conclusion first, then the reasons, then evidence as far as they choose to go. They may have five minutes. If your answer is on slide 14, they will never see it.

The pyramid

Barbara Minto's structure:

  1. One governing thought at the top: your answer or recommendation.
  2. Three or so supporting arguments beneath it, each a full statement.
  3. Evidence beneath each argument.

Ideas at each level must be of the same kind, must not overlap, and together must cover the ground. Consultants call this mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

Opening: situation, complication, question, answer

A reliable way to begin any memo or presentation:

  • Situation: what the reader already agrees with.
  • Complication: what has changed or gone wrong.
  • Question: the question this raises.
  • Answer: your governing thought.

"Sahyadri launches two welding lines a year. The last four launches each started production three to five weeks late, costing about ₹15 lakh a week. How can we launch on time? By validating robot and control programs virtually before installation, which should recover four weeks per launch for an investment of ₹1.5 crore."

Four sentences, and a busy reader knows what you want and why.

Headlines that say something

A slide or section title should state the conclusion. "Commissioning analysis" is a label. "Virtual commissioning pays back in 14 months" is a message. Someone reading only the titles in order should get the whole argument.

Writing

  • One idea per paragraph, stated in its first sentence.
  • Short sentences with the verb near the front.
  • Numbers with units and a comparison: "6 weeks against 10 today".
  • Cut anything the reader does not need in order to decide.
  • State what you want from the reader and by when.

Presenting to senior people

  • Send a short pre-read, and assume half have not read it.
  • State the decision you need in the first minute.
  • Expect to be interrupted. Answer the question asked, briefly, then return.
  • If you do not know, say so and say when you will.
  • Bring the detail as backup and use it only when asked.
  • End by restating the decision and next steps.

A one-page memo outline

  1. Recommendation, in two sentences.
  2. Why now.
  3. Three reasons, each with its key number.
  4. Cost, risk and the downside case.
  5. Alternatives considered and why they were rejected.
  6. Decision requested and next steps.

An ethical problem of the kind that actually arises, with a deadline attached.

The situation

Isha Gokhale is a solution consultant at Meridian. A ₹6 crore deal with a pharmaceutical equipment maker is due to close this week, the last of the quarter. The customer's quality head has said that automated generation of validation documents is essential for regulatory compliance and wants to see it in Friday's final demo.

That capability is on Meridian's roadmap for release in about nine months. A prototype exists that works with one prepared data set. The account executive, Tushar, asks Isha to demo the prototype without saying that it is unreleased. "They will not go live for a year anyway. By then it will be there. Everybody does this. If we lose this deal the team misses its number and there will be cuts." Isha's manager is travelling and has sent a message: "Do what it takes to close."

Your questions

  1. Who is affected and how?
  2. Apply the three lenses and the practical tests.
  3. Identify the rationalisations in Tushar's request and answer each.
  4. What options does Isha have besides complying or refusing?
  5. Write what she should say to Tushar.
Model answer

1. The customer: it would commit ₹6 crore and its regulatory compliance to a capability that does not exist, in an industry where validation failures can stop shipments. The quality head personally. Meridian: revenue now, against the risk of a dispute, a lost renewal, a damaged reference and possible legal exposure for misrepresentation. Delivery colleagues who inherit the promise. Tushar and the team, who face the number. Isha: her credibility with this customer and her own standards.

2. Consequences: a short-term gain against a serious and likely harm, since roadmap dates slip. Duties: presenting an unreleased prototype as a product feature is deception about something the customer has called essential. Character: it is the first step in becoming someone whose demos cannot be trusted. Tests: she would not be comfortable if the customer saw the conversation with Tushar. She would not accept it as the customer. She is being asked to stay silent. All point the same way.

3. "They will not go live for a year": the customer is deciding now and is entitled to decide on facts. A year is also when the gap would be found. "It will be there by then": that is a hope. If he is confident, a contractual commitment costs nothing. "Everybody does this": common practice does not change what it is, and regulated customers do not forgive it. "There will be cuts": real pressure, and it does not turn a misrepresentation into a favour to colleagues. "Do what it takes": the manager did not know the specifics. Isha should not assume he meant this.

4. Show the prototype openly as a preview of a planned capability, with the planned release date. Offer a written roadmap commitment, or contract terms that tie part of the payment to delivery of the feature. Propose an interim solution using existing capabilities and a services package. Bring product management into the call to speak to the plan. Ask the customer whether a committed date ahead of their go-live meets the need. Escalate to her manager with the facts before Friday.

5. "I want this deal as much as you do, and I think we can still close it this week. I will not show the prototype as if it were released. If it slips, we lose the account and the reference, and with a regulated customer it could be far worse. Here is what I can do: show it as a preview, with product management confirming the date, and we offer a contractual commitment before their go-live. That gives the quality head something she can defend internally. If we believe our roadmap, that costs us nothing. Can we call our manager together now and agree it?"

What the case teaches. The choice is seldom between complying and refusing. Prepare an alternative that serves the legitimate goal, state your limit plainly, and frame it in terms of the business's own interest. People who do this consistently become the ones customers and executives trust, and that reputation is a large part of what a senior career is built on.
