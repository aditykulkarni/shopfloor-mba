Entrepreneurship is taught in every MBA because starting something, inside a company or outside, uses every other subject at once and under uncertainty.

Idea and opportunity

An idea is a possible solution. An opportunity exists when a specific group of customers has a problem painful enough to pay for, you can reach them, and you can solve it at a cost below what they will pay. Most ideas are not opportunities, and the work of the first months is finding out which this one is.

Good questions to ask of an idea

  • Who exactly has this problem, and how do they deal with it today?
  • How often does it occur and what does it cost them?
  • Why has nobody solved it? Perhaps it cannot be solved profitably. Perhaps something has just changed that makes it possible.
  • Why you? What do you know or have that others do not?
  • How would customers find out about it and buy it?

The business model canvas

Alexander Osterwalder's canvas describes a business in nine blocks on one page.

Every block of a new venture's canvas is a guess. The method is to mark the riskiest guesses and test them in order, as in product discovery.

A beachhead market

Bill Aulet's advice mirrors Geoffrey Moore's from Month 4: choose one narrow group of customers who talk to each other and share the problem, and win it completely before widening. A start-up that tries to serve three segments has too little of everything for each.

Types of venture

  • Small business: serves a local or niche market, grows steadily, funded by its own cash and loans. Most new firms, and many good ones.
  • Scalable start-up: aims at a large market with a product that can grow much faster than its costs. Needs outside capital and accepts a high chance of failure.
  • Corporate venture: a new business inside an established company, with access to its resources and exposure to its constraints.

They need different funding, people and expectations. Confusing them is a common source of disappointment.

Effectuation

Research on experienced founders by Saras Sarasvathy finds that they often start from what they have (who they are, what they know, whom they know), commit only what they can afford to lose, bring in partners who commit early, and let the goal take shape as they learn. It is a useful counterweight to the picture of the founder with a fixed plan.

In your job

You see unsolved problems at customers every week, which is where most business-to-business ventures begin. Whether or not you ever start a company, the habit of asking "who has this problem, how often, at what cost?" is the one product and strategy roles look for.

Focus concentrates scarce resources.

A venture is financially sound when each unit of what it sells earns more than it costs, and when it can fund itself until that adds up to enough. Both can be checked on one page.

Unit economics

Choose the unit: a customer, an order, a machine, a subscription. Then ask:

  1. What does one unit bring in?
  2. What does it cost to deliver, in variable costs?
  3. What did it cost to acquire?
  4. How long until the acquisition cost is recovered?

These are contribution margin from Month 1 and lifetime value and acquisition cost from Month 4. A business whose unit economics are negative does not become profitable by growing. It loses money faster.

Cash runway

Runway in months = cash in the bank ÷ net cash burned per month

A venture with ₹60 lakh and a monthly burn of ₹5 lakh has twelve months. Raising money takes three to six months, so the search must start with at least that much runway left. Start-ups die of running out of cash more often than of anything else.

How venture capital thinks

A venture fund expects most of its investments to fail or return little, and a few to return many times the amount invested. So each investment must have the potential to become very large. A sound business that could reach ₹20 crore of revenue is a fine thing to own and a poor fit for venture capital. Taking venture money commits a company to chasing rapid growth and a sale or listing within several years.

Valuation and dilution

Post-money valuation = pre-money valuation + amount invested

Investor's share = amount invested ÷ post-money valuation

A founder raises ₹2 crore at a pre-money valuation of ₹8 crore. Post-money is ₹10 crore, the investor owns 20% and the founders 80%.

Two years later the company raises ₹10 crore at a pre-money valuation of ₹40 crore. Post-money is ₹50 crore. The new investor owns 20%. Everyone else is diluted by a fifth: the founders hold 80% × 0.8 = 64%, and the first investor 16%.

The founders' share has fallen and its value has risen from ₹8 crore to ₹32 crore. Dilution is acceptable when the money raised increases the value of the whole by more than the share given up. An employee option pool, usually set aside before a round, dilutes further.

Terms beyond price

Investors' shares usually carry preferences, the most important being the right to get their money back first in a sale. In a modest exit that can leave founders with much less than their percentage suggests. Read the terms, and take advice.

In your job

If you join a start-up, the same arithmetic applies to your share options. Ask what percentage of the company they represent, at what price, over what vesting period, and what investors receive before ordinary shareholders in a sale. An option grant quoted only as a number of shares tells you nothing.

Arrive rested, with routines fresh.

Most ventures fail because nobody wanted what they made. The cure is to find that out early, at small cost. The same applies to new initiatives inside large firms.

Customer development

Steve Blank's principle: there are no facts inside the building, so get out. Before building, hold structured conversations with potential customers to test the problem, then the solution, then the willingness to pay.

Cheap tests, in order of cost

  1. Problem interviews: do people have this problem, and what do they do about it now?
  2. A simple page or brochure describing the offer, to see who asks for more.
  3. A manual version of the service for a handful of customers.
  4. Pre-orders, deposits or letters of intent.
  5. A small pilot in one location or with one customer, paid if at all possible.

For each test, write down beforehand what you expect and what result would make you stop or change. Without that, every result looks like encouragement.

Pivot or persevere

When evidence contradicts a key assumption, change that part of the model and keep what was learned: a different customer segment, a different problem for the same customers, a different channel or pricing. A pivot is a new hypothesis, held to the same standard of evidence.

Affordable loss

Decide at the outset how much time and money you can lose without lasting harm, and structure the venture so that the first tests fit inside that. Keeping a job while testing an idea at weekends is a legitimate and common route.

Innovation inside established companies

Large firms have customers, capital, brand and distribution. They also have features that kill new ventures.

  • Planning systems that demand forecasts for something nobody can forecast.
  • Measures suited to the mature business, such as margin and utilisation, applied to a business with neither yet.
  • Risk aversion. A failed project harms a career more than a missed opportunity does.
  • The core business's claim on resources. People and budget drift back to where today's revenue is.
  • Counter-positioning. The venture threatens existing products, as Month 6 described.

Approaches that help: a separate team with its own measures and a protected budget, funding released in stages against evidence in the way a venture investor would, a senior sponsor, and a clear path for a successful venture to move into the main business.

Being an intrapreneur

Someone who develops a new offering inside a company uses the same method: find a real customer problem, test it cheaply with one or two friendly customers, gather evidence, then ask for resources with results in hand. Evidence persuades executives far better than enthusiasm, and a small success gives your sponsor something to defend.

In your job

A packaged demo, a reusable value calculator or a repeatable workshop format is an internal product. Build one, try it at three accounts, measure what it changed, and present the result. That is intrapreneurship at a scale available to you now, and it is visible to exactly the people who decide promotions.

Before running a test of a venture idea, you should write down:

  1. A five-year financial forecast
  2. What you expect and what result would make you change course
  3. The company name

Otherwise any outcome can be read as support.

New ventures inside large companies often fail because:

  1. Large companies lack money
  2. Their employees lack ideas
  3. The parent's planning, measures and priorities are designed for the mature business

The systems that run the core starve the new.

The "affordable loss" principle says to:

  1. Limit early commitment to what you can lose without lasting harm
  2. Maximise expected return
  3. Avoid all risk

It keeps you able to try again.

A small venture with clear numbers. The question is what to find out before spending.

The situation

Kshitij Pawar, an engineer with a full-time job, has developed an energy drink with a contract bottler and has designed a compact refrigerated vending machine to sell it. He is deciding whether to build 50 machines. All figures are his estimates.

  • A machine costs ₹1.8 lakh to build.
  • A can sells for ₹60 and costs ₹22 to produce and deliver.
  • Site owners such as gyms and offices take 15% of sales.
  • Electricity, restocking visits and maintenance cost ₹4,000 a month per machine.
  • He expects each machine to sell 40 cans a day.
  • He has savings of ₹12 lakh that he is prepared to risk.

Friends who tried the drink said they liked it. A vending operator has told him informally that a new, unknown brand typically sells 10 to 20 cans a day in a machine.

Your questions

  1. Calculate contribution per can, monthly profit per machine and payback at 40 cans a day.
  2. Repeat at 15 cans a day. What is the break-even volume per day?
  3. What would 50 machines cost, and how does that compare with what he can afford to lose?
  4. List the assumptions the venture rests on. Which two are riskiest?
  5. Design the cheapest tests of those two.
  6. Is this a small business or a scalable start-up, and why does it matter?
Model answer

1. Site commission is ₹9 a can. Contribution = 60 − 22 − 9 = ₹29. At 40 cans a day, about 1,200 a month: 1,200 × 29 = ₹34,800, less ₹4,000, leaves ₹30,800 a month. Payback on ₹1.8 lakh is under six months.

2. At 15 a day, 450 a month: ₹13,050 less ₹4,000 is ₹9,050 a month, and payback is about 20 months. Break-even is 4,000 ÷ 29 = 138 cans a month, under five a day. The machine covers its running costs at low volumes, and the return on the capital depends heavily on volume.

3. ₹90 lakh, more than seven times his affordable loss, before working capital for stock. He cannot and should not start with 50.

4. Assumptions: strangers will buy the drink at ₹60, repeatedly. Volume per machine. Sites can be secured at 15%. The machine works reliably at the stated cost. Restocking cost. Product cost at small volumes. Food-safety and other licences are obtainable. The two riskiest: repeat demand for an unknown brand, and daily volume per machine. Friends' praise is the weakest form of evidence, and the operator's figure of 10 to 20 a day contradicts his own.

5. Demand can be tested without any machine. Place the drink in an ordinary cooler at three gyms on a sale-or-return basis for a month, at the planned price, and count sales and repeat purchases week by week. Cost: a few thousand rupees of stock. Machine economics: build or rent one or two machines and run them at the best two sites for two months, logging cans a day, faults and restocking time. Decide in advance: for example, proceed to ten machines only if average sales exceed 25 a day by the second month and are not falling.

6. As described, it is a small business that grows by adding machines, each needing capital, so growth is limited by cash and it is unlikely to attract venture investors. That is fine, and it changes the plan: fund from profits and perhaps equipment loans, expand site by site. It also raises a strategic question. Is the venture really about the drink or about the machine? If the drink sells, existing retailers and vending operators are a far cheaper channel. If the machine is the innovation, it could carry other brands. Testing them separately will show where the value lies.

What the case teaches. Work out the unit economics at the pessimistic volume as well as the hoped-for one. Find the assumption that everything depends on, and test it for a thousandth of the cost of the full plan. Keep the first steps inside what you can afford to lose.

An MBA classroom runs on cases: messy situations with incomplete information and a decision to make. After eight months you have every tool. This session puts them in an order you can use under pressure.

Ten steps

  1. What is the decision, and whose is it? State it as a question that has alternative answers.
  2. What are the real alternatives? Include doing nothing, and look for a third option that buys flexibility.
  3. What does the industry look like? Five forces, profit pools, how it is changing. (Month 6)
  4. What do customers value, and which customers? Segments, willingness to pay, alternatives. (Month 4)
  5. Where does the company's advantage come from, if it has one? And will the decision strengthen or dilute it? (Month 6)
  6. What are the economics? Contribution, fixed costs, break-even, cash flows, NPV, sensitivity, break-even assumption. (Months 1 and 3)
  7. Can the organisation deliver it? Constraint, capacity, variability, supply. (Month 5)
  8. Will people do it? Owner, incentives, structure, change plan. (Month 7)
  9. What could go wrong? Competitor response, macro shocks, execution risk, worst case and whether it can be survived. (Months 2 and 6)
  10. What do you recommend? Answer first, three reasons, the main risk and how to handle it, the first steps. (Month 7)

You will rarely need all ten at equal depth. Decide early which two or three the decision turns on, and spend your time there.

Habits of good analysis

  • Form a hypothesis early and test it. An hour of open-ended fact gathering produces less than ten minutes aimed at a specific question.
  • Do the rough numbers first. An estimate to one significant figure tells you whether a detailed model is worth building.
  • Find the break-even assumption. What must be true for this to be the right choice, and how plausible is it?
  • Ask what you would need to believe to choose the other option.
  • Separate facts, estimates and opinions, in your own work and in what you are told.
  • Take a position. A list of considerations is not a recommendation.

In a case discussion or interview

  • Restate the question and ask one or two clarifying questions.
  • Lay out how you will approach it before diving in.
  • Think aloud with structure, do the arithmetic cleanly, and say what each number implies.
  • End with a recommendation, the main risk and next steps, even if time is short.

What the case method trains

The content of any single case is soon forgotten. What stays is the habit of deciding with incomplete information, defending the decision to capable people who disagree, and changing your mind when they are right. You can practise that with colleagues on real decisions at work.

In your job

Use the ten steps to prepare for the next executive meeting at a customer. You will not present them. They will tell you which of the customer's questions you cannot yet answer.

  1. Calculate NPV
  2. State the decision to be made and who makes it
  3. Describe the company's history

Everything else is organised around the decision.

A recommendation is most persuasive when it includes:

  1. Every fact from the analysis
  2. No mention of risk
  3. The answer, the main reasons, the main risk and the first steps

Decision makers need to act and to know what could go wrong.

Finding the "break-even assumption" means identifying:

  1. The value of a key input at which the decision would change
  2. The year the project repays its cost
  3. The lowest possible price

It shows how wrong you can be before the answer flips.

Ideas that recurred

  • Profit is a thin residual, so small changes in price, cost or volume move it a great deal.

  • Think at the margin and ignore sunk costs.
  • The constraint governs the system.
  • Variability must be buffered by inventory, capacity or time.

  • Value is relative to the customer's alternative.

The situation

Sahyadri Auto Components makes welded steel structures for vehicles. The CEO, Nandini Rao, must recommend to the board whether to build a plant for aluminium battery trays for electric vehicles. You are asked to prepare the board paper. Amounts are in ₹ crore.

The company

  • Revenue 1,200. EBIT 96. Debt 300 at 9% interest. Equity 500. Free cash flow about 40 a year.
  • 60% of revenue comes from parts specific to combustion-engine vehicles. That revenue is expected to shrink by about 3% a year as electric vehicles gain share.
  • Sahyadri's loan agreements require interest cover of at least 2.5 times.
  • Discount rate 12%. Tax rate 25%.

The market

  • Battery trays for Indian-built electric vehicles are a market of about 800 a year, growing roughly 30% a year from a small base. Forecasts vary widely.
  • Four suppliers are active, two of them subsidiaries of global tier-1 firms with aluminium experience.
  • Two vehicle makers, both existing Sahyadri customers, have given letters of intent covering revenue of 96 a year. The letters are not binding.
  • Vehicle makers expect yearly price reductions of 2 to 3%.

The proposal

  • Capital cost 120, plus working capital of 20, both at the start. The plant is depreciated at 15 a year. After seven years it could be sold for its book value of 15, and the working capital is recovered.
  • Full capacity gives revenue of 240. Planned ramp: 60 in year 1, 150 in year 2, 240 from year 3 to year 7.
  • Contribution margin 30%. Fixed costs 25 a year.
  • Losses in the plant reduce the group's tax bill.

Operations and people

  • Aluminium welding and sealing are new to Sahyadri. The plan assumes 70% OEE from year 2. New aluminium lines elsewhere have often started near 50%.
  • The manufacturing engineering head, Farhan Shaikh, has run virtual commissioning on two steel lines with good results.
  • No one in the company has run an aluminium plant. The plant heads' bonuses depend on this year's output and cost.
  • An alternative design exists: a first phase with capital of 70 and working capital of 10, capacity for revenue of 130, fixed costs of 16 and depreciation of 8.75 a year, with space left to add a second line later.

Reference figures

Discount factors at 12%: year 1 is 0.893, year 2 is 0.797, year 7 is 0.452. The sum of the factors for years 3 to 7 is 2.874.

Your task

  1. State the decision and the real alternatives.
  2. Assess the industry Sahyadri would be entering and the one it is in.
  3. Calculate the after-tax cash flows and NPV of the full plant on the plan as given.
  4. Recalculate with steady-state revenue of 180 instead of 240.
  5. Check what borrowing to fund the plant does to interest cover in year 1.
  6. Assess the operational and people risks.
  7. Write a recommendation of not more than one page for the board.
Model answer

1. Decision and alternatives. Should Sahyadri enter battery trays now, and on what scale? The alternatives are the full plant, the phased plant, a partnership or licence with an experienced aluminium firm, and staying out. Staying out is not neutral: 60% of revenue is on a slow decline.

2. Industry. Sahyadri's present industry has strong buyer power and intense rivalry, and now faces substitution as drivetrains change. That explains an EBIT margin of 8%. Battery trays share the same powerful buyers, with yearly price-downs. Rivalry is lighter for now, with four suppliers, and entry barriers are higher because of process knowledge and capital. Growth is fast and will attract entrants, so today's 30% contribution margin is unlikely to last. The move does not escape buyer power. It replaces a shrinking position with a growing one, in which Sahyadri would start behind two experienced competitors.

3. Base case. Year 0: −140. Year 1: contribution 18, less fixed costs 25, EBITDA −7. EBIT −22, tax saving 5.5, cash flow −1.5. Year 2: contribution 45, EBITDA 20, EBIT 5, tax 1.25, cash flow 18.75. Years 3 to 7: contribution 72, EBITDA 47, EBIT 32, tax 8, cash flow 39. Year 7 also brings 35 from the plant's sale and working capital. Present value: −1.5 × 0.893 + 18.75 × 0.797 + 39 × 2.874 + 35 × 0.452 = −1.3 + 14.9 + 112.1 + 15.8 = 141.5. NPV is about +1.5. The plan, if everything goes right, only just earns the cost of capital.

4. Lower volume. At revenue of 180: contribution 54, EBITDA 29, EBIT 14, tax 3.5, cash flow 25.5. Present value of years 3 to 7 is 73.3. Total: −1.3 + 14.9 + 73.3 + 15.8 = 102.7. NPV is about −37. The letters of intent cover only 96, so 180 is far from a pessimistic case. A price-down of 2 to 3% a year, not included in the plan, would cut contribution further.

5. Financing. Interest is now 27 and cover is 96 ÷ 27 = 3.6. Borrowing all 140 at 9% adds 12.6 of interest. In year 1 the plant loses 22 at EBIT level, so group EBIT is about 74 and interest 39.6: cover of 1.9, a breach of the 2.5 requirement, before any decline in the existing business. Even borrowing half leaves cover at about 2.2. The full plant cannot be funded by debt.

6. Operations and people. Year 2 revenue of 150 assumes 70% OEE on a process nobody in the company has run. At 50% OEE the plant could not make the planned volume, the ramp would slip a year, and each year of delay costs roughly the difference between a 39 and a 19 cash flow. Learning-curve effects mean the first years will carry extra scrap and rework. There is no experienced leader and no incentive for existing plant heads to release good people. Farhan's virtual commissioning experience is a real asset for de-risking the launch, and it does not replace process expertise.

7. Recommendation to the board.

"We recommend entering battery trays through a first phase of ₹80 crore, with a second line to follow only when binding orders justify it. We do not recommend the full ₹140 crore plant now.

Three reasons. First, we need a position in electric vehicle structures: 60% of our revenue is in slow decline, and both customers offering letters of intent are ones we must keep. Second, the full plant earns an NPV of about ₹1.5 crore if everything goes to plan and loses about ₹37 crore if volume settles at three quarters of capacity, while firm indications cover only 40%. It would also breach our loan terms in its first year. Third, the first phase has a similar NPV on its own plan, about ₹3 crore, loses roughly ₹22 crore if volume reaches only 100, can be funded half from our own cash flow with interest cover staying near 2.9, and keeps the option to double capacity once demand and our process capability are proven.

Conditions. Convert the letters of intent into contracts with volume commitments or tooling contributions before capital is committed. Recruit a plant head with aluminium experience and tie his and the programme team's incentives to the ramp. Validate the line virtually before installation and plan year-1 output at 50% OEE. Seek a technical partner for welding and sealing know-how.

The main risk is that we enter too small and a competitor takes the volume. We accept it because the second line can be added within a year, and because the larger risk is committing ₹140 crore against uncommitted demand with a process we have yet to master."

Notes on the phased option. Year 1: contribution 18 less fixed 16 gives EBITDA 2, EBIT −6.75, tax saving 1.7, cash flow 3.7. Years 2 to 7 at revenue 130: contribution 39, EBITDA 23, EBIT 14.25, tax 3.6, cash flow 19.4. Year 7 adds 18.75. Present value: 3.3 + 15.5 + 55.9 + 8.5 = 83.1 against 80, so NPV is about +3. At revenue of 100 the yearly cash flow is 12.7 and NPV is about −22. Funding 40 from cash and 40 from debt adds 3.6 of interest: year-1 cover is about 89 ÷ 30.6 = 2.9.

What the case teaches. The numbers alone say "marginal". The strategy says "necessary". The financing and operations say "not at this scale". A good recommendation holds all three, finds the option that keeps the upside while limiting the loss, and states the conditions under which it would change. That is the judgement this course has been building, and it is what senior management consists of.

A short session on what to do with the result.

If you reached your target

  • Send scores to your chosen schools according to the current reporting rules.
  • Move your study time to essays and recommendations.
  • Note the score's validity period. It gives you some years in which to apply.

  • Check the waiting period and limits on retakes before booking.
  • Remember that schools assess the whole application. A score slightly under a school's average with strong experience and clear goals is competitive at many programmes.

Keeping the skills

The reasoning habits from this strand, such as finding the gap in an argument, checking sufficiency before calculating and reading data before reading answers, are the same ones the rest of the course used. They do not expire with the score.

Your real Verbal score is well below both mocks, and the other sections match. The most likely explanation and response are:

  1. You never understood Verbal; start again
  2. A test-day factor such as fatigue or timing; review pacing and consider a retake
  3. The exam was unfair; do nothing
  4. Mocks are useless

One section far off practice level usually reflects conditions on the day.

Once you have a score in your target schools' usual range, extra effort is best spent on:
