Every result in the last six months was produced by people. Organisational behaviour studies why they do what they do. It has fewer formulas than finance and matters more to anyone who will manage others.

Why this subject is hard for engineers

Machines respond the same way to the same input. People respond to how they interpret a situation, and they interpret it differently. The research gives tendencies, which is enough to manage far better than by instinct.

Four ideas that hold up

Herzberg's two factors. The things that cause dissatisfaction are different from the things that create motivation. Poor pay, bad supervision and unfair policies make people unhappy, and fixing them only brings people to neutral. Motivation comes from achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth and the work itself. A manager who only fixes complaints gets a team that is not unhappy and not engaged.

Expectancy theory. People put in effort when they believe three things: effort will produce performance, performance will produce a reward, and they value the reward. If any link is broken, effort drops. "It makes no difference how hard I work" is a broken second link, and no amount of bonus money fixes the first or third.

Self-determination. People have lasting needs for autonomy (some control over how they work), competence (getting better at something that matters) and relatedness (belonging). Work that provides these produces motivation from within, which lasts longer and yields better work on complex tasks than pressure from outside.

Goal setting. Specific, difficult goals that people accept produce higher performance than vague ones such as "do your best", provided there is feedback. This is among the best-supported findings in the field. Its risk is tunnel vision: people hit the stated goal and neglect what was left out.

Fairness

People compare what they give and get with what others give and get. Perceived unfairness, in pay or in how decisions are made, damages motivation more than low absolute rewards. How a decision is reached and explained often matters more than the outcome. People accept disappointing decisions made through a process they regard as fair.

A diagnostic for a manager

When someone is underperforming, ask in this order before concluding it is attitude:

  1. Do they know exactly what is expected?
  2. Do they have the skill and the tools?
  3. Do they get feedback on how they are doing?
  4. Does performing well lead to anything they value?
  5. Is something getting in the way: workload, conflicting priorities, a process?

Most performance problems are found in the first three.

In your job

You may already mentor interns. Try the diagnostic on the next task you hand over: state the outcome precisely, check the skill, and agree when you will review it. That is what managing is, practised small.

Incentives work. That is the danger. People do what is rewarded, including when it is not what was intended.

Rewarding A while hoping for B

Steven Kerr's classic paper catalogues organisations that hope for one behaviour and pay for another. Examples you have already met in this course:

  • Hoping for profitable sales, paying on revenue: discounting rises (Month 4).
  • Hoping for plant output, measuring every machine's utilisation: inventory piles up (Month 5).
  • Hoping for low total cost, measuring purchase price: distant, risky sourcing (Month 5).
  • Hoping for teamwork, paying individual bonuses: people withhold help.
  • Hoping for long-term value, rewarding the quarter: deals pulled forward at a discount.

When behaviour in an organisation looks irrational, look at what is measured and rewarded. It usually turns out to be rational.

Why strong incentives are risky

  • Multiple tasks. If a job has measurable and unmeasurable parts, strong rewards for the measurable part pull effort away from the rest. Pay teachers on test scores and they teach to the test.
  • Gaming. People find the cheapest way to move the number.
  • Crowding out. Paying for something people did out of interest or duty can reduce their own motivation to do it.
  • Short horizons.

Strong individual incentives suit simple, measurable, independent work. Complex, collaborative work with hard-to-measure quality is better served by fair base pay, modest team-based variable pay, and managers who know what good work looks like.

Performance management

The annual appraisal is the least important part. What matters is the conversation through the year.

  • Clear expectations, agreed in advance.
  • Frequent feedback that is specific, about behaviour and results, and given soon after the event. "In yesterday's workshop you answered the CFO's question about payback before she finished asking it, and she stopped engaging" is useful. "Be more of a listener" is not.
  • Regular one-to-one meetings. Andrew Grove argued these are the highest-leverage use of a manager's time: an hour that improves a subordinate's work for the next two weeks.
  • No surprises at appraisal time.

Giving difficult feedback

State the observation, the effect, and what you would like instead. Ask for their view. Do it privately and promptly. Kim Scott's shorthand is to care personally and challenge directly. Withholding criticism to be kind leaves people to fail without knowing why.

In your job

Ask how you are measured, and how your manager is measured. Then look for where the measures pull against what the customer needs. You cannot always change them, but you can stop being surprised by the behaviour they produce, including your own.

Most important work is done in teams and most important decisions are made in groups. Both can be much better or much worse than the individuals in them.

What makes teams work

Research on real teams points repeatedly to the same conditions, summarised well by Richard Hackman: a clear and compelling purpose, the right mix and number of people, clear norms, enough resources, and coaching at the right moments. Talent alone predicts little.

Psychological safety is Amy Edmondson's term for a shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask questions and admit mistakes. Teams with it report more errors, because they surface them, and they learn faster. It is different from being comfortable. Safe teams argue more, about the work.

Size. Coordination cost rises with the number of links between members: 5 people have 10 links and 10 people have 45. Small teams move faster.

Stages. New teams commonly pass through forming, storming, norming and performing. Conflict in the second stage is normal. A leader's job is to get the team through it.

Biases in individual judgement

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people rely on mental shortcuts that produce predictable errors.

AnchoringThe first number mentioned pulls later estimates towards itPrice negotiations, budget rounds
ConfirmationPeople seek and believe evidence for what they already thinkPilot evaluations
OverconfidenceRanges are too narrow and forecasts too certainBusiness cases, sales forecasts
Planning fallacyProjects are expected to finish sooner and cheaper than similar past ones didImplementation schedules

How groups make it worse

  • Groupthink. A cohesive group suppresses doubt to keep harmony.
  • Deference. Once the senior person speaks, others align.
  • Polarisation. Discussion among like-minded people pushes them to a more extreme view.
  • Shared information bias. Groups discuss what everyone already knows and neglect what only one member knows.

Practices that help

  • Write first. Have people record their estimates or views independently before any discussion.
  • Senior people speak last.
  • Pre-mortem. Gary Klein's method: imagine it is a year from now and the project has failed. Each person writes down why. It gives permission to voice doubts.
  • Outside view. Ask how long similar projects actually took, before looking at this one's plan.
  • Assign a devil's advocate, or set up a second team to argue the opposite case.
  • Separate deciding from proposing.

In your job

Run a pre-mortem before your next large demo or proposal. Ten minutes of "it failed, why?" with the account team will surface the missing stakeholder or the untested data set while there is still time.

The situation

Meridian's India solution-consulting team has twelve consultants. A year ago its manager, Kunal Bhosale, introduced a bonus worth up to 30% of salary, paid on the bookings of deals each consultant personally supported.

Results after a year. Bookings rose 8%. But several things changed. Consultants compete for the largest deals and avoid small ones and early-stage discovery work. Two experienced consultants no longer help juniors with demo environments. Reusable demo assets are not being shared. The win rate on mid-sized deals fell from 30% to 22%. Three juniors have resigned. Delivery teams complain that customers were promised capabilities that needed heavy customisation.

Kunal thinks the answer is to raise the bonus to 40% and add a penalty for lost deals.

Your questions

  1. Use Kerr's idea to state what is being rewarded and what is hoped for.
  2. Explain each side effect in terms of the incentive.
  3. Why would Kunal's proposed change make things worse?
  4. Redesign the pay and measures for this team.
  5. What should Kunal do apart from pay?
Model answer

1. Rewarded: individual credit for large bookings this year. Hoped for: a team that wins as many good deals as possible, develops its people, builds reusable assets and sets up customers for successful delivery.

2. Chasing big deals and avoiding small ones: payoff is proportional to deal size, so effort goes where the expected bonus is highest. Avoiding discovery: early work is far from a booking and may be credited to someone else. No help for juniors and no sharing of assets: helping a colleague costs time and can reduce one's own share. Falling mid-size win rate: these deals get the least experienced people with the least support. Resignations: juniors get neither deals nor coaching. Over-promising: the bonus pays at booking and nothing ties it to whether the customer succeeds.

3. A higher bonus strengthens every one of these effects. A penalty for lost deals adds a reason to avoid uncertain opportunities altogether and to blame others. Both increase the pull towards the measured part of the job.

4. Lower the variable share to perhaps 15 to 20%. Base most of it on team bookings and team win rate, so that helping a colleague pays. Add a part tied to something after the sale, such as customer go-live or first-year renewal, to restrain over-promising. Assign deals by rotation and development need and not by competition. Recognise contributions to shared assets and mentoring in promotion decisions, which is where they can be judged, since they are hard to measure in a formula.

5. Talk to the three who left and the juniors who remain. Hold regular one-to-ones. Pair seniors with juniors on mid-sized deals and make coaching an explicit expectation of the senior role. Review a sample of promises made in deals with the delivery team each quarter. And admit to the team that the plan had effects he did not intend, which will do more for trust than any new scheme.

What the case teaches. For interdependent, hard-to-measure work, strong individual incentives damage the parts of the job that are not counted. When a plan misfires, turning it up is the instinctive reaction and usually the wrong one.

Culture

Edgar Schein describes culture at three levels.

  1. Artefacts: what you can see. Offices, dress, rituals, how meetings run.
  2. Espoused values: what the organisation says it believes. Posters and value statements.
  3. Underlying assumptions: what people take for granted without discussion. This is the real culture.

The gap between levels two and three is where to look. A firm that says it values candour and where nobody contradicts the director has an underlying assumption about hierarchy that outweighs the stated value.

Culture forms from what has worked in the past and from what leaders pay attention to, measure, reward and tolerate. It changes slowly, mainly when leaders consistently behave differently and when measures and promotions back that up. Announcing new values changes nothing.

Reading a culture quickly

  • Who gets promoted, and for what?
  • What happens to someone who brings bad news?
  • How are decisions really made: in the meeting or before it?
  • What stories do people tell about the company's past?
  • What is never discussed?

Power

Power is the capacity to get things done through others. It has several sources.

  • Position: formal authority, control of budgets and appointments.
  • Expertise: knowing what others need to know.
  • Information and network: being connected across groups that do not otherwise talk.
  • Control of scarce resources: people, money, access to a key customer.
  • Personal credibility: a record of delivering and of dealing straight.

Engineers often regard organisational politics as illegitimate. Jeffrey Pfeffer's argument is that politics is simply how decisions get made when people have different interests and information, and that capable people who refuse to engage lose to less capable people who do. The ethical line is about ends and honesty, not about whether to build influence.

The informal organisation

Every company has a network of trust and advice that differs from the chart. Some people with modest titles are consulted by everyone. They are worth identifying, since a change they support spreads and a change they doubt stalls.

National and professional cultures

Working across countries adds differences in how directly people speak, how hierarchy is treated, how decisions are reached and how time is regarded. Erin Meyer's work maps these. The practical rule is to ask, early and openly, how a team prefers to disagree and decide, instead of assuming your own norms.

You said at the start that you want to lead transformation. This session is the centre of that ambition. Most transformation programmes fall short, and the reasons are well known.

Why change is hard

  • Loss aversion. People feel what they will lose now more than what they might gain later.
  • Competence. Experts in the old way become beginners in the new one.
  • Rational interests. Some people really are worse off: less control, less status, more transparency.
  • The dip. Performance usually falls before it rises, as the Month 1 reading passage on electric motors described.
  • Change fatigue from earlier initiatives that faded.

Resistance is information. It tells you who loses, what is unclear, and what was wrong with the plan.

Kotter's eight steps

  1. Establish urgency. A reason that matters to the people who must change.
  2. Build a guiding coalition with enough power and credibility.
  3. Form a clear vision of the future state.
  4. Communicate it, far more than seems necessary, and through behaviour.
  5. Remove obstacles: structures, measures and managers that block the change.
  6. Create short-term wins that are visible and unambiguous.
  7. Consolidate and keep going. Do not declare victory early.
  8. Anchor the change in the culture through promotions, measures and stories.

His finding was that failure usually came from skipping a step, especially the first two and the sixth.

The individual's view

The ADKAR model says each person needs five things in order: awareness of why, desire to take part, knowledge of how, ability to do it in practice, and reinforcement to keep doing it. Training supplies knowledge only. Programmes that train thoroughly and neglect desire and reinforcement get people who know how to use a system and do not.

Why digital transformations stall

Surveys by consulting firms consistently report that a minority of transformation programmes meet their goals, and BCG's work on AI attributes about 70% of the effort to people and process, 20% to technology and data, and 10% to algorithms. The recurring causes:

  • The technology goes live and the way of working does not change.
  • Pilots succeed with enthusiasts and are never scaled to the sceptical majority.
  • Line managers are measured on this quarter's output and absorb none of the transition cost willingly.
  • The sponsor moves on.
  • Benefits are never measured, so nobody can show it worked.

What works

  • A sponsor in the line organisation who owns the result.
  • Changing measures and roles at the same time as the tools.
  • Making the new way easier than the old, and removing the old where possible.
  • Early wins chosen for visibility, with numbers.
  • Involving respected informal leaders in the design.
  • Staying past go-live until use and benefits are established.

In your job

This is the durable skill your career report identified. Tool configuration is becoming cheap. Getting an organisation to work differently and proving the benefit is scarce. Seek out the adoption and value-realisation phases of projects even though the kick-off and go-live are more visible.

  2. Why will more training on its own fail?
  3. Who are the key people, and what does each stand to gain or lose?
  4. Write a 90-day plan for Nandini.
  5. How would you answer the CFO?
Model answer

1. Kotter: there was no urgency that mattered to the plants, the guiding coalition was IT and one executive who has gone, obstacles such as the old spreadsheets and the output-only bonus were left in place, no short-term wins were shown, and victory was declared at go-live. Star model: a process and technology change was made while structure, rewards and people's roles stayed as they were. The system was implemented and the organisation was not changed.

2. Training supplies knowledge. The engineers know how to use the system. They lack desire, since it is slower for small changes and nobody requires it, and reinforcement, since the spreadsheet route still works and their managers do not care. More training tells them something they already know.

3. Plant heads: they gain fewer launch errors and less obsolete stock in the long run, and they bear slower changes and extra effort now, with no reward. Manufacturing engineers: they lose a tool they control and gain traceability that is of more use to others than to them. Design engineers: similar. Quality and programme managers: clear winners, since they suffer from untracked changes. The CFO: wants evidence. An informal leader among the Chennai engineers is probably shaping opinion there.

4. Days 1 to 30: Nandini names a line executive, such as the operations head, as owner. Measure a baseline now: change lead time, errors traced to wrong revisions, obsolete stock from late changes. Meet the Chennai engineers and fix the top complaints about small changes, with a fast path for minor ones. Days 31 to 60: choose one upcoming vehicle programme and run every change for it through the system, with the plant head's agreement and support on site. Add a change-quality measure to plant heads' scorecards. Days 61 to 90: publish the comparison between that programme and the baseline. Set a date after which spreadsheet changes are not accepted for new programmes. Bring the vendor back for adoption support with fees tied to usage.

5. "The money already spent is sunk. The question is whether the remaining spend is worth it. We do not yet have the evidence, which is our own failure, and we will have it in 90 days from one programme with a proper baseline. If changes on that programme are faster and cleaner, we extend and switch off the old route. If they are not, we stop. I am asking for a small, bounded amount to find out."

What the case teaches. A system going live is an IT milestone. The business result needs an owner in the line, measures that reward the new behaviour, removal of the old route and evidence. This is the work that remains scarce and valuable as tools get easier.

Sort your errors

  1. Concept: you did not know how. Remedy: relearn the topic, then do ten questions on it.
  2. Process: you knew how and misread, miscalculated or answered a different question. Remedy: a fixed routine, such as rereading the question stem before confirming.
  3. Timing: correct but too slow, or rushed at the end. Remedy: practise the two-and-a-half-minute cut-off.
  4. Trap: chose an attractive wrong answer. Remedy: for each one, write why the trap looked right.

Five questions on common process errors

A tank is 40% full. After 30 litres are added it is 55% full. The capacity of the tank is:

  1. 75 litres
  2. 150 litres
  3. 200 litres
  4. 54.5 litres

30 litres is 15% of capacity.

The price of a part rises from ₹80 to ₹100. By what percentage would it have to fall to return to ₹80?

  1. 25%
  2. 20%
  3. 22.5%
  4. 80%

20 ÷ 100. The base is now 100.

If x is 25% less than y, then y is what percentage more than x?

  1. 25%
  2. 20%
  3. 30%
  4. 33.3%

x = 0.75y, so y = 1.333x.

A question asks for the number of units that are NOT defective. 1,200 units were made and 3.5% were defective. The answer is:

  1. 1,158
  2. 42
  3. 1,196
  4. 1,242

42 are defective. Read which quantity is asked for.

Data sufficiency. What is the value of x + y? (1) 2x + 2y = 18. (2) x − y = 1.

  1. C: both together
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. A: statement 1 alone
  4. E: not sufficient

Halving statement 1 gives the answer directly. Choosing "both" is the classic trap.

Five questions, then a change plan for something real.

A company organised by product divisions will tend to be good at:

  1. Sharing specialists efficiently
  2. Accountability for each product's results
  3. Uniform standards across the group
  4. Minimising duplicated roles

Each division owns its outcome, with some duplication.

A firm announces "collaboration" as a value while promoting only individual top sellers. Staff will conclude that:

  1. Collaboration matters most
  2. Values are unimportant everywhere
  3. The posters are correct
  4. Individual results are what is really valued

Promotions reveal the underlying assumptions.

In ADKAR, which element does classroom training mainly provide?

  1. Knowledge
  2. Desire
  3. Reinforcement
  4. Awareness

Knowing how is different from wanting to and from doing it day to day.

A change programme's sponsor leaves six months in. The greatest risk is that:

  1. The software stops working
  2. The budget rises
  3. The guiding coalition loses the power to remove obstacles
  4. Training is cancelled

Without a powerful owner, competing priorities win.

Leaving the old spreadsheet route available after a new system goes live mainly:

  1. Reduces risk with no cost
  2. Removes the reason to switch and keeps two processes running
  3. Improves data quality
  4. Speeds adoption

People keep the familiar route while it works.

Work assignment: a one-page change plan

  1. Choose a change you would like to see in your own team or in a customer project you know. Keep it small enough to be real.
  2. State the urgency in terms that matter to those who must change.
  3. List who gains and who loses, and who the informal leaders are.
  4. Name the obstacles: measures, old routes, habits.
  5. Define one short-term win with a number and a date.
  6. Say what would have to change in measures or roles for it to stick.
