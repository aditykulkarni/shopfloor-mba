Business strategy asks how to win in one industry. Corporate strategy asks which industries and activities a company should own at all, and what the parent adds to them.

The test

Shareholders can diversify by buying shares in many companies, cheaply. So a company that owns several businesses must do something for them that investors could not do themselves. Porter's three tests for entering a new business:

  1. Attractiveness: is the industry structurally attractive, or can it be made so?
  2. Cost of entry: does the price of getting in leave any of the future profit?
  3. Better off: will the new unit, or the existing ones, gain an advantage from being together?

Most failed diversifications skip the third.

How a parent adds value

  • Shared activities: a common sales force, platform, plant or purchasing.
  • Transferred skills: a capability built in one business applied to another.
  • Better management: capital allocation, performance discipline, talent development. Many Indian business groups rely on this, together with access to capital and reputation.

The costs of being together are real as well: head-office overhead, slower decisions, and cross-subsidy of weak units by strong ones. Markets often value a diversified group below the sum of its parts, which is called the conglomerate discount.

Portfolio tools

The BCG growth-share matrix sorts businesses by market growth and relative market share into stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs, with the advice to move cash from cows to stars and selected question marks. It is simple to the point of being misleading, since share does not guarantee profit, but it captures one lasting idea: a group should deliberately move capital from mature businesses to those with a future, and many do not. Units tend to reinvest what they earn whether or not they have the best opportunities.

Vertical integration, revisited

Own a stage when markets work badly there: when a supplier could hold you to ransom after you have made investments specific to it, when coordination needs are intense, or when the stage holds knowledge you cannot afford to lose. Otherwise buy, and keep the flexibility.

In your job

Industrial software vendors have grown mainly by related diversification through acquisition, adding simulation, manufacturing, electronics and data products to a common platform. The "better off" test for each is whether customers get more from the combination than from separate products. As a consultant you often carry the burden of proving that it does.

  1. Always pursue market share
  2. Actively reallocate cash from mature businesses to those with better prospects
  3. Sell all low-growth units

Capital allocation across units is the parent's main job.

Acquisitions are the fastest way to change a company and the easiest way to destroy value. Studies over decades find that on average the sellers gain and the buyers' shareholders gain little or lose.

The arithmetic of a deal

Value created for the buyer = present value of synergies − premium paid − integration costs

A target is worth ₹100 crore on its own. The buyer pays ₹130 crore, a 30% premium. Synergies have a present value of ₹40 crore. The buyer gains 40 − 30 = ₹10 crore, before integration costs. If synergies are worth ₹25 crore, the buyer has lost ₹5 crore on the day it signed.

The premium is certain and paid upfront. The synergies are uncertain and come later. That asymmetry is the whole problem.

Kinds of synergy

  • Cost synergies: removing duplicated overhead, plants and purchasing. Usually achievable and fairly quick.
  • Revenue synergies: cross-selling each firm's products to the other's customers. Usually overestimated and slow.
  • Financial synergies: lower cost of capital or tax. Usually small.

A sound rule is to count cost synergies at a discount, revenue synergies at a heavy discount, and to subtract the business lost during the disruption of integration.

Why buyers overpay

  • The winner's curse. In an auction the winner is the bidder with the most optimistic estimate.
  • Overconfidence and deal momentum once advisers and executives have invested months.
  • Incentives. Advisers are paid on completion. Executives of larger companies are paid more.
  • Synergies assumed to be free, when they need investment and management time.

Integration

Most of the value is won or lost after signing. The common failures are losing key people in the first months, customers leaving during the confusion, incompatible systems and processes, and culture clashes that nobody planned for. Speed and clarity help: who has which job, which product lines continue, what customers should expect.

Alternatives to buying

Alliances, joint ventures, licences and minority stakes give access to a capability with less capital and less control. They suit situations where the future is uncertain or where only part of the other firm is wanted.

When acquisitions work

  • Small, frequent deals in a field the buyer knows, with a practised integration method.
  • Buying a product or technology that the buyer's existing sales channel can take to many customers.
  • Consolidating a fragmented industry with real cost synergies, which is the private equity "buy and build" approach.

In your job

If your employer is acquired or acquires, the first hundred days decide careers. The people who do well are those who make themselves useful to the integration: they know the customers, the products and where the two fit. Volunteer for that work.

Many of the most valuable companies are platforms. The term is used loosely in industrial software, so it is worth being exact about what a platform is and when the economics really apply.

Pipelines and platforms

A pipeline business makes something and sells it: inputs flow in, a product flows out. A platform business creates value by enabling interactions between groups, such as buyers and sellers or users and developers, and takes a share.

Network effects

  • Direct: each user makes the product more valuable to other users of the same kind. A file format everyone can open.
  • Indirect: more users on one side attract more on the other. More customers on a platform attract more application developers, whose applications attract more customers.

Network effects are a source of power, as Helmer's list showed. Last week's passage gave the two limits: multi-homing and effects that saturate.

Two kinds of platform

  • Transaction platforms match parties: marketplaces, payment systems.
  • Innovation platforms provide a base on which others build complementary products: operating systems, cloud infrastructure, and engineering platforms with partner applications.

Hard problems for any platform

  • Starting. Neither side will join until the other is there. Common answers: subsidise one side, bring your own products or users as the first supply, or start in a narrow niche.
  • Pricing. One side is often charged little or nothing because its presence is what the other side pays for.
  • Governance. What partners may do, what the owner reserves for itself, how quality is kept up. A platform owner that competes with its own partners discourages them from investing.
  • Openness. Open interfaces attract complements and weaken lock-in. Closed ones do the reverse.

Is an industrial software suite a platform?

Partly. A shared data model and common services across design, simulation and manufacturing give economies of scope and high switching costs. Those are real and valuable, and they are not network effects. True platform economics appear only where third parties build and sell on top, or where connecting more companies, such as a manufacturer and its suppliers, makes the system more valuable to each. Industrial markets are also smaller and more specialised than consumer markets, so their network effects are weaker and more local. Calling a product suite a platform does not give it platform economics.

Ecosystems

An ecosystem is the set of firms whose products must work together for the customer to get value: vendor, resellers, integrators, hardware makers, content providers. Strategy within one means deciding which parts to own, which to leave to partners, and how to keep partners profitable enough to stay.

Buy. The founders want 120. On its own plan the start-up is worth about 70. Meridian believes 10% of its 1,000 customers would adopt the product at ₹8 lakh a year, at an 80% gross margin, with that income growing 5% a year. Integration would cost 15. Meridian's discount rate is 12%.

Build. Three years and 40 of development cost, with perhaps a 50% chance of producing something competitive.

Partner. Resell Tolerant's product for a 30% share of the revenue, with no upfront cost. Tolerant would remain free to partner with Meridian's competitors.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the yearly gross profit from cross-selling and its present value as a growing perpetuity.
  2. What is the acquisition worth to Meridian, and what is its net value at a price of 120?
  3. How sensitive is that to the adoption rate?
  4. Roughly compare the build option's expected value with buying. Use the same cross-selling income if the build succeeds, delayed three years.
  5. What does the partner option earn, and what is its weakness?
  6. What do you recommend?
Model answer

1. 100 customers × ₹8 lakh = ₹8 crore of revenue, ₹6.4 crore of gross profit. Present value = 6.4 ÷ (0.12 − 0.05) = ₹91 crore.

2. Standalone 70 + synergy 91 − integration 15 = 146. At a price of 120 the net value is +26. Note where the value goes: Meridian pays a premium of 50 over standalone value and keeps 26 of a 76 net synergy. The sellers capture two thirds of the synergy.

3. At 5% adoption the synergy halves to 46, value to Meridian is 101 and the deal loses 19. Break-even is 91 − 26 = 65 of synergy value, which is about 7% adoption. The deal depends on a cross-selling assumption, the kind that is usually overestimated.

4. If the build succeeds, the same 91 arrives three years later, worth 91 ÷ 1.12³ = 65 today. With a 50% chance, the expected value is 32.5, less 40 of cost: about −7. Building also leaves Meridian without a product for three years while competitors move, and it gets none of Tolerant's existing customers or team.

5. Partnering earns 30% of ₹8 crore, ₹2.4 crore a year, worth about 34 as a growing perpetuity, with no investment and no integration risk. Its weakness is strategic: Meridian does not control the roadmap, rivals can offer the same capability, and if the product succeeds Tolerant's price goes up or a competitor buys it.

6. Buy, but not at 120 on these assumptions. Negotiate towards 100 to 105, or structure part of the price as payments tied to adoption targets, which moves the cross-selling risk onto the sellers who are confident about it. Test the adoption assumption first with a short exclusive resale period among 50 customers. If the founders refuse both, partner with a right of first refusal on any sale.

What the case teaches. Acquisition value is the standalone value plus synergies minus costs, and the seller will try to price in the synergies. Find the break-even assumption and then structure the deal so that whoever believes it carries the risk.

No strategy is carried out in a vacuum. Demand, interest rates, exchange rates and policy move around every plan, and senior managers are expected to have a view on them.

Gross domestic product

GDP is the value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period. It can be added up by spending:

GDP = Consumption + Investment + Government spending + (Exports − Imports)

  • Consumption by households is the largest part in most economies, India included.
  • Investment covers factories, machines, infrastructure and housing. It is smaller and far more volatile.
  • Government spending on goods and services.
  • Net exports.

Nominal GDP is measured at current prices. Real GDP removes inflation and is what "growth" refers to. This is Month 3's real and nominal distinction at national scale.

What drives long-run growth

Output per person rises through more capital per worker, better skills, and productivity: getting more from the same inputs through technology and better organisation. Over decades productivity does most of the work. Small differences compound: at 7% a year an economy doubles in about ten years, and at 3.5% in twenty.

The business cycle

Around the trend, economies move through expansion, peak, contraction and recovery. Cycles are irregular and hard to forecast.

Investment swings far more than consumption. When demand slows a little, firms with spare capacity stop buying equipment altogether. This is the bullwhip effect of Month 5 at the level of the economy, and it is why capital-goods and automation suppliers experience booms and droughts.

India's structure

India's economy is led by domestic consumption and services. Manufacturing has been a smaller share of output than in the East Asian economies at a similar stage, and raising that share is a stated aim of policy. Interest rates enter every discount rate you used in Month 3. They are set, in large part, by a central bank reacting to inflation.

Inflation

Inflation is a general rise in prices, usually measured by the consumer price index. Moderate, steady inflation is manageable. High or unpredictable inflation distorts decisions, since nobody can tell whether a price rise reflects scarcity or just falling money value, and it erodes savings.

Causes fall into two groups. Demand-pull: spending grows faster than the economy can produce. Cost-push: input costs rise, as with oil, food or a weaker currency. India is exposed to the second through energy imports and food prices.

Monetary policy

The Reserve Bank of India targets consumer inflation of 4%, within a band of 2 to 6%. Its main tool is the repo rate, the rate at which it lends to banks.

  • Inflation too high: raise the rate. Borrowing costs more, spending and investment slow, price pressure eases.
  • Growth too weak with inflation under control: cut the rate.

Changes take months to work through to loan rates and longer to affect activity. Check the current rate and inflation figures when you need them. The mechanism matters more than any number here.

How rates reach a business

  • Cost of debt on floating-rate loans, and so interest cover.
  • Discount rates: higher rates raise WACC and lower the value of future cash flows. Long-payback projects and growth companies are hit hardest.
  • Customer demand for anything bought on credit: vehicles, housing, machinery.
  • Working capital cost.
  • Exchange rates, covered next.

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is government spending and taxation. A fiscal deficit is spending above revenue, funded by borrowing. Spending on infrastructure, production incentives and tax changes shift demand between sectors directly. Heavy government borrowing can push up interest rates for private borrowers, an effect called crowding out. The Union Budget each February is the main event for reading the direction of fiscal policy.

Real interest rates

What matters for investment is the real rate: the nominal rate minus expected inflation. A 9% loan with 6% inflation costs about 3% in real terms. The same loan with 2% inflation costs about 7%.

In your job

When rates rise, customers lengthen approval chains and raise hurdle rates. Proposals with staged investment and early benefits keep moving, and proposals with large upfront payments stall. Offer a structure that fits the conditions.

Manufacturers buy and sell across borders. Exchange rates and trade policy change their costs and their competitors without anyone on the shop floor doing anything differently.

Exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. If the rate moves from ₹90 to ₹95 per euro, the rupee has depreciated.

When the rupee depreciatesEffect
Exporters paid in foreign currencyGain: each euro earned converts to more rupees
Importers of materials, components and machinesLose: costs rise
Firms with foreign-currency loansLose: repayments cost more
Domestic producers competing with importsGain: imports become dearer
InflationRises, through imported energy and goods

A firm that invoices an export order at €10 lakh receives ₹9.0 crore at 90 and ₹9.5 crore at 95, a gain of ₹50 lakh for no extra effort. A firm importing robots at the same value pays ₹50 lakh more.

What moves exchange rates

  • Inflation differences. A currency with higher inflation tends to depreciate over time.
  • Interest rate differences. Higher rates attract capital and support the currency.
  • Trade and capital flows. Imports of oil, exports of services, foreign investment.
  • Central bank action and market sentiment.

Short-term movements are close to unpredictable. Businesses manage the exposure instead of forecasting it.

Managing currency risk

  • Natural hedging: match the currency of costs and revenues, for instance by buying inputs in the currency you sell in.
  • Forward contracts: fix today the rate for a future transaction.
  • Pricing clauses that pass currency changes to the customer.

Trade and comparative advantage

Countries gain from trade by specialising in what they produce at the lowest opportunity cost. The gains are real in aggregate and unevenly spread, which is why trade policy is contested. Tariffs protect domestic producers and raise costs for domestic users of the protected goods.

Industrial policy in India

The main instruments in recent years have been production-linked incentive schemes, which pay manufacturers in selected sectors a percentage of incremental sales, along with tariff changes, infrastructure spending and efforts to simplify regulation. A wider trend runs alongside: multinational firms spreading supply chains across more countries, often called China-plus-one.

What to ask of any such policy from a business viewpoint: Does it change the long-term economics of producing in India, or only the next few years? Are the gains in assembly alone or deeper in components? Which of your customers are building capacity because of it? Read current scheme details and results from official sources, since they change.

In your job

  2. ₹4,500 billion
  3. ₹3,300 billion
  4. ₹3,000 billion

2,000 + 800 + 350 − 150.

A minister claims a new training scheme cut youth unemployment, citing a fall in the rate since it began. Which would be most useful in assessing the claim?

  1. The scheme's budget
  2. How youth unemployment changed over the same period in comparable regions without the scheme
  3. How many trainers were hired

A comparison separates the scheme from the general trend.

Work assignment: a two-page strategy memo

  1. Choose a business unit, product line or small company you know well.
  2. Page one: the diagnosis. Industry forces, where profit sits, the unit's advantage or lack of one, and the main challenge.
  3. Page two: a guiding policy in two sentences, five coherent actions, and what the unit should stop doing.
  4. Add one adverse scenario and say what would be done first if it occurred.
  5. Keep it. Strategy memos like this are what consulting interviews and MBA case discussions ask you to produce aloud.
