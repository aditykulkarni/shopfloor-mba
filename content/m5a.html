You know operations from the engineering side. This month teaches it the way a general manager sees it: as a system that turns capital and time into money, with a small number of measures that explain most of its behaviour.

Three measures of any process

  • Flow rate (throughput): units completed per unit of time.
  • Flow time: how long one unit takes from entry to exit, including waiting.
  • Inventory: units inside the process at any moment.

Little's law

Inventory = Flow rate × Flow time

A plant completes 100 units a day and holds 600 units of work-in-progress. Average flow time is 6 days. The law holds for any stable process: a factory, a hospital, an engineering change queue, a sales pipeline. It needs no assumption about how the process is organised.

Its practical message: for a given output, cutting inventory and cutting lead time are the same act. And since inventory is working capital, lead-time reduction releases cash, which connects the shop floor to Month 1's balance sheet.

Capacity and the bottleneck

Each resource has a capacity: the most it can process per unit of time. The resource with the lowest capacity is the bottleneck, and it sets the capacity of the whole process.

StationCapacity, units per hourUtilisation at demand of 40
A: stamping6067%
B: welding4589%
C: finishing5080%

Process capacity is 45 an hour. If demand is 40, actual flow rate is 40, because a process produces the lower of demand and capacity. Welding is the bottleneck at 89% utilisation.

Cycle time and takt time

Cycle time is the time between completed units. At 45 an hour it is 80 seconds. Takt time is available time divided by customer demand: at 40 an hour it is 90 seconds. A line must run at a cycle time at or below takt to meet demand.

Utilisation

Utilisation = Flow rate ÷ Capacity

Only the bottleneck should be near full utilisation. Running non-bottleneck stations flat out builds inventory in front of the bottleneck and adds nothing to output. Measuring every machine on utilisation is one of the most common and costly errors in plant management.

In your job

A flow simulation finds the bottleneck. The managerial step is to say what it means: which investment raises plant output, which does not, and how much working capital sits in queues. Lead with those three conclusions.

Eliyahu Goldratt turned bottleneck logic into a management method. His novel The Goal is on almost every MBA operations reading list, and it is a quick read.

The core claim

Every system has at least one constraint that limits its performance against its goal. For a company the goal is to make money, now and in the future. Improving anything other than the constraint is, at best, wasted effort.

Five focusing steps

  1. Identify the constraint.
  2. Exploit it: get the most from it as it is. Never let it wait for material, run it through breaks, inspect parts before it and not after, cut its changeovers.
  3. Subordinate everything else to it. Other resources work to the bottleneck's pace.
  4. Elevate it: add capacity by investing, only after steps 2 and 3.
  5. Repeat. When the constraint moves, go back to step 1.

The order matters. Most companies jump to step 4 and buy equipment, when steps 2 and 3 cost nearly nothing.

What a bottleneck hour is worth

An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole plant, and it cannot be recovered. If the welding station makes 45 units an hour and each unit carries a contribution of ₹2,000, an hour of bottleneck downtime costs ₹90,000 when demand exceeds capacity. An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is worth nothing in output terms.

This overturns cost accounting intuitions. Paying overtime, an extra operator or a premium for fast maintenance at the bottleneck looks expensive per hour and is cheap compared with ₹90,000.

Throughput accounting

Goldratt uses three measures: throughput (sales minus truly variable costs, essentially contribution), inventory (money tied up in the system) and operating expense (money spent to turn inventory into throughput). Decisions are judged by their effect on these three, in that order of priority.

Drum, buffer, rope

The scheduling method that follows: the bottleneck sets the pace (drum). A time buffer of work in front of it protects it from upstream disruption. Material is released into the plant only at the rate the bottleneck consumes it (rope), which caps work-in-progress.

Constraints outside the factory

The constraint is often not a machine. It may be market demand, a policy, an approval step or a scarce specialist. In engineering organisations the constraint is frequently one expert or one review board, which Week 18's case examines.

In your job

When a customer asks for a business case, find out whether the area you improve is the plant's constraint. If it is, the benefit is extra contribution on every unit gained, which is large. If it is not, the benefit is limited to cost and inventory. The same technical result can be worth ten times more in one plant than in another.

After identifying the constraint, the next step is to:

  1. Buy more capacity
  2. Raise utilisation everywhere
  3. Get the most out of the constraint as it is

A machine rarely produces what its nameplate promises. OEE measures the gap and sorts it into causes, and the economics of capacity explain why the gap matters so much.

Overall equipment effectiveness

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality

  • Availability: running time ÷ planned production time. Lost to breakdowns and changeovers.
  • Performance: actual output ÷ output at ideal speed during running time. Lost to slow running and short stops.
  • Quality: good units ÷ total units. Lost to scrap and rework.

A machine with 90% availability, 85% performance and 98% quality has an OEE of 0.90 × 0.85 × 0.98 = 75%. Three respectable numbers multiply into a quarter of capacity lost. An OEE around 85% is widely quoted as world class for discrete manufacturing, and many plants run between 50 and 65%.

Hidden capacity

Raising OEE at a bottleneck from 60% to 72% increases its output by 20% with no capital. This is the "exploit" step in practice, and it is why OEE at the constraint is the most valuable operating number in a plant. OEE at non-constraints matters much less.

The economics of utilisation

Fixed costs are spread over output. A plant with fixed costs of ₹60 crore making 1,00,000 units carries ₹6,000 of fixed cost per unit. At 80,000 units it carries ₹7,500. This is the operating leverage of Month 1 from the operations side, and it explains the pressure to fill capacity.

That pressure has to be balanced against two things. Producing more than demand turns into inventory. And as Week 18 shows, running close to full capacity with variable demand creates long queues.

Adding capacity

Capacity usually comes in large steps: a new line, a new shift. Three timing strategies exist.

  • Lead: build ahead of demand. Never short, often underused.
  • Lag: build after demand is proven. Efficient, loses sales.
  • Match: add in small increments. Needs flexible equipment or outsourcing.

The choice is an NPV decision under uncertainty, like the robotic cell case in Month 3. Flexible and modular capacity is worth a premium because it lets a firm follow demand in smaller steps.

Line balancing

Dividing work so each station's time is close to takt time minimises idle time. Perfect balance leaves no slack for variability, so real lines need small buffers or a little spare capacity at each station.

In your job

When a simulation shows that a line needs fewer stations or one shift less, state the result as capital avoided and fixed cost per unit. Those are the terms a capacity decision is approved in.

A station with capacity for 10 jobs an hour, receiving 9 an hour on average, should have no queue. In practice it has a long one. Understanding why is the most important idea in operations after the bottleneck.

The cause

Arrivals and processing times vary. When three jobs arrive together, two wait. When no job arrives, the station sits idle, and that idle time cannot be saved up for later. Lost capacity is gone, while a queue persists. So variability creates waiting even when average capacity exceeds average demand.

The waiting-time formula

For a single station, a good approximation (Kingman's formula) is:

Average wait ≈ [u ÷ (1 − u)] × [(CVa² + CVp²) ÷ 2] × average processing time

Here u is utilisation, CVa is the coefficient of variation of the time between arrivals and CVp is that of processing times. Recall from Month 2 that the coefficient of variation is standard deviation divided by mean.

What it shows

Take a processing time of 10 minutes with both coefficients equal to 1, which is typical of random arrivals.

Utilisationu ÷ (1 − u)Average wait, minutes
50%110
80%440
90%990
95%19190
98%49490

Waiting time explodes as utilisation approaches 100%. Going from 80% to 95% utilisation raises output by under a fifth and multiplies waiting by almost five.

Three levers

  1. Utilisation. Keep some spare capacity. It looks wasteful on a utilisation report and it buys short lead times.
  2. Variability. Halving both coefficients of variation cuts waiting to a quarter. Standard work, reliable machines, levelled schedules and fewer product variants all do this.
  3. Processing time. Faster processing lowers both the last term and utilisation.

The managerial conclusion

A company must choose between high utilisation and fast response unless it reduces variability. Firms that want both, and do nothing about variability, get long lead times and expediting. This single trade-off explains hospital waiting lists, engineering backlogs, call-centre queues and late projects as well as factory lead times.

In your job

Engineers and consultants are resources too. A team loaded to 95% has very long turnaround on any new request, for exactly this reason. When a customer's engineering change process is slow, check the utilisation of the approvers before proposing a workflow tool.

Variability that is not removed has to be absorbed. There are exactly three ways to absorb it, and choosing between them is a strategic decision.

The three buffers

Hopp and Spearman's law of variability buffering: variability in a production system will be buffered by some combination of inventory, capacity and time.

BufferWhat it looks likeWho uses it
InventoryFinished goods and safety stock so customers do not waitConsumer goods, spare parts
CapacitySpare machines, overtime, flexible labourEmergency services, fast-response suppliers
TimeThe customer waits: quoted lead times, backlogsMachine builders, engineering to order, most public services

A company does not get to choose whether to buffer. If it refuses to hold inventory or spare capacity, the buffer appears as lead time, and customers pay it. Lean production is best understood as reducing variability first, so that all three buffers can shrink together.

Pooling

Combining separate sources of variability reduces the total, because highs in one place offset lows in another.

  • Pooled queues. One queue feeding several servers waits far less than separate queues.
  • Pooled inventory. One central warehouse needs less safety stock than several regional ones. For independent demands of similar size, safety stock grows with the square root of the number of locations pooled: four warehouses merged into one need about half the safety stock.
  • Flexible resources. Cross-trained operators and machines that can run several products pool capacity.
  • Common parts and late differentiation. A shared platform pools demand across variants. Finishing a product to order from a common base postpones the point at which forecasts must be right.

The price of pooling

Central stock is further from customers. Flexible machines cost more than dedicated ones. Cross-training takes time. Pooling is worth its cost when variability is high.

Variability from inside

Much variability is self-inflicted: large batches, long changeovers, unreliable equipment, rework, end-of-month sales pushes and engineering changes released in bulk. These are cheaper to remove than to buffer.

In your job

Product platforms and modular bills of material are pooling strategies carried out in PLM. Their operational value, which is less safety stock, shorter lead time and better availability, can be estimated with the square-root logic and belongs in the business case next to the engineering benefits.

For three decades manufacturers were urged to hold as little inventory as possible. Stock was described as waste: it tied up capital, hid quality problems and delayed the discovery of defects. Firms that adopted just-in-time delivery reported large gains, and the practice spread from car makers to industries with quite different conditions.

A series of disruptions to global supply chains prompted a reappraisal. Critics argued that lean systems had been made fragile, and that firms should return to holding substantial buffers. This criticism misreads the original method. Its pioneers reduced inventory only after years of work to stabilise their processes and to develop suppliers located close to their plants. Low stock was the result of low variability. Many imitators copied the result without the cause, cutting inventory while sourcing from distant suppliers with long and uncertain lead times.

The appropriate response to disruption is therefore unlikely to be a general return to large stocks. It is to match the buffer to the variability actually faced, which will mean more protection for some items and no change for most.

Inventory decisions balance the cost of holding stock against the cost of ordering or running out. Three simple models cover most situations.

Why hold inventory at all

  • Cycle stock: because ordering or producing in batches is cheaper than one at a time.
  • Safety stock: because demand and supply are uncertain.
  • Pipeline stock: goods in transit or in process.
  • Seasonal and speculative stock.

Holding cost

The yearly cost of holding a unit includes the cost of capital, storage, insurance, obsolescence and damage. For manufactured goods it typically runs at 20 to 30% of the item's value a year, more than most managers assume.

Economic order quantity

EOQ = √(2 × annual demand × cost per order ÷ holding cost per unit per year)

Annual demand is 12,000 units. Each order costs ₹2,000 to place and receive. Holding a unit for a year costs ₹30.

EOQ = √(2 × 12,000 × 2,000 ÷ 30) = √16,00,000 ≈ 1,265 units

That is about 9.5 orders a year. The same formula sets production batch sizes, with set-up cost in place of order cost. Its key lesson for manufacturing: halving the set-up cost cuts the best batch size by about 30%. This is the reasoning behind quick changeover.

Safety stock and reorder point

Safety stock = z × standard deviation of daily demand × √lead time in days

z depends on the service level wanted: 1.65 for 95% and 2.33 for 99%. With daily demand averaging 48 and a standard deviation of 20, a 9-day lead time and a 95% service level:

Safety stock = 1.65 × 20 × 3 = 99 units

Reorder point = 48 × 9 + 99 = 531 units

Two lessons. Safety stock rises with the square root of lead time, so shorter and more reliable lead times reduce it directly. And moving from 95% to 99% service raises safety stock by 41%. The last few points of availability are expensive.

The newsvendor model

For a single period with uncertain demand, such as a seasonal product or a last-time buy, order up to the point where:

Probability of having enough = cost of understocking ÷ (cost of understocking + cost of overstocking)

If a unit short loses ₹300 of margin and a unit left over loses ₹100, stock enough to cover demand 75% of the time. When running out costs far more than leftover stock, as with a cheap part that can stop a line, the right service level is very high.

The situation

At Sahyadri Auto Components, every engineering change order must be reviewed by one manufacturing change coordinator before release. Programme managers complain that changes sit for more than a week. The IT head proposes a new workflow system at ₹60 lakh.

Your questions

  2. Estimate the average wait before review begins, in working hours and days.
  3. What would the wait be if arrivals were as regular as review times, with a coefficient of variation of 1?
  4. Templates and a pre-check by the originator could cut review time to 1.5 hours. What is the wait then, with bursty arrivals?
  5. Would the workflow system alone solve the problem? What do you recommend?
Model answer

1. Capacity is 40 ÷ 2 = 20 a week. Utilisation is 18 ÷ 20 = 90%.

2. Wait ≈ (0.9 ÷ 0.1) × ((2² + 1²) ÷ 2) × 2 hours = 9 × 2.5 × 2 = 45 working hours. That is more than a full working week, which matches the complaints.

3. 9 × ((1 + 1) ÷ 2) × 2 = 18 hours, a little over two days. Burstiness alone accounts for 27 of the 45 hours.

4. Capacity becomes 26.7 a week and utilisation 67.5%. Wait ≈ (0.675 ÷ 0.325) × 2.5 × 1.5 = 2.08 × 3.75 = 7.8 hours, about one day.

5. A workflow system that only routes and tracks the same work does not change utilisation, variability or review time, so the queue stays. Add a second trained reviewer for peak periods: pooling two reviewers on one queue cuts waiting sharply. Then consider the system, specified to support those changes.

A tool that tracks work more efficiently will shorten lead time substantially only if:

  1. It has a good interface
  2. It reduces utilisation, variability or processing time at the constraint
  3. Everyone is trained
  4. It runs in the cloud

Those are the only terms in the waiting-time formula.
