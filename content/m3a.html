A rupee today is worth more than a rupee next year, because today's rupee can be invested. Everything in finance is built on that sentence.

Future value

FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ

₹100 invested at 10% becomes ₹110 after one year, ₹121 after two and ₹133.1 after three. Interest earns interest. This is compounding.

A shortcut: money doubles in roughly 72 ÷ r years. At 12% it doubles in about six years. At 6% it takes twelve.

Present value

Run the same formula backwards.

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ

What is ₹1 crore received in five years worth today, if you could earn 12% elsewhere? 1 ÷ 1.12⁵ = 1 ÷ 1.762 = ₹0.567 crore. You should be indifferent between ₹56.7 lakh now and ₹1 crore in five years. The rate used is called the discount rate, and 0.567 is the discount factor.

Valuing a stream of cash

Discount each amount and add. Present values can be added because they are all measured at the same date.

A project pays ₹50 lakh at the end of each of the next three years. At 12%: 50 × 0.893 + 50 × 0.797 + 50 × 0.712 = 44.6 + 39.9 + 35.6 = ₹120.1 lakh. The ₹150 lakh of nominal benefits is worth ₹120 lakh today.

What this changes

Month 1's payback and ROI treated all rupees alike. Discounting penalises benefits that arrive late and costs that arrive early. A project with slow, back-loaded benefits can look fine on three-year ROI and poor on present value.

Where the rate comes from

The discount rate is the return available on the best alternative use of the money at similar risk. It is an opportunity cost. For a company it is the return its investors require, which Week 11 calculates. Until then, treat 12% as a reasonable working figure for an Indian manufacturer.

Many cash flows are regular: rent, subscriptions, loan payments, maintenance fees. Regular streams have shortcuts that save you from discounting year by year.

Perpetuity

A payment of C every year for ever, starting in one year, is worth:

PV = C ÷ r

₹10 lakh a year for ever at 10% is worth ₹100 lakh. If the payment grows at rate g each year:

PV = C ÷ (r − g)

₹10 lakh next year growing 4% a year, at 10%, is worth 10 ÷ 0.06 = ₹167 lakh. This growing perpetuity formula values the long-term future of whole companies, as Week 12 shows. Note how sensitive it is: raise g to 6% and the value jumps to ₹250 lakh.

Annuity

A payment of C every year for n years is worth:

PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r

The term multiplying C is the annuity factor. At 12% the factors are:

Loans

A loan is an annuity seen from the lender's side. The equal instalment that repays a loan P over n periods at rate r per period is:

Instalment = P × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ]

A ₹10 lakh loan at 12% a year, repaid monthly over one year, has a monthly rate of 1% and 12 periods. The instalment is about ₹88,850. Total paid is ₹10.66 lakh. In the first month ₹10,000 of the instalment is interest and the rest repays principal. The interest share shrinks every month.

Compounding more than once a year

12% a year compounded monthly is 1% a month, which is 1.01¹² − 1 = 12.68% effective a year. Always compare rates on the same effective basis.

The cost of supplier credit

A supplier offers 2% off if you pay in 10 days and not 45. Paying ₹98 now avoids paying ₹100 in 35 days. The return is 2 ÷ 98 = 2.04% for 35 days, which is about 21% a year. A firm that borrows at 10% should take the discount every time.

The discount rate is the most argued-over number in any investment case. Understanding what goes into it lets you argue well.

Three ingredients

  • Time preference. Even with no risk and no inflation, people want compensation for waiting.
  • Inflation. Money loses purchasing power.
  • Risk. Uncertain cash flows are worth less than certain ones.

The yield on a 10-year Indian government bond bundles the first two. It has been roughly 6.5 to 7.5% in recent years; check the current figure when you need it. Anything riskier must offer more.

Nominal and real

A nominal rate includes inflation. A real rate removes it.

(1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)

With a nominal rate of 12% and inflation of 5%, the real rate is 1.12 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 6.7%. A common approximation is nominal minus inflation, which gives 7%.

The rule is consistency. Discount nominal cash flows, which include price rises, at a nominal rate. Discount real cash flows, in today's prices, at a real rate. Mixing them is one of the commonest errors in business cases: benefits held flat in today's rupees, discounted at a nominal 12%, are undervalued.

Risk belongs in the rate or in the cash flows, once

You can reflect risk by raising the discount rate or by lowering the expected cash flows. Doing both double-counts. Good practice: use honest expected cash flows, which are probability-weighted averages and not best cases, and a rate that matches the project's risk.

Different projects, different rates

A cost-saving project on an existing line is low risk, because the volume already exists. A new product for a new market is high risk. Many companies use one hurdle rate for everything. That makes safe projects look worse than they are and risky ones better.

Country and currency

Rupee cash flows are discounted at rupee rates. Euro cash flows at euro rates, which are lower because inflation is lower. A 12% rupee hurdle and an 8% euro hurdle can describe the same real return.

The situation

Sahyadri Auto Components is choosing how to buy a manufacturing engineering software suite. The vendor offers:

  • Perpetual: ₹3.0 crore now, plus maintenance of ₹0.45 crore at the end of each year.
  • Subscription: ₹1.1 crore at the end of each year, maintenance included.

Sahyadri's discount rate is 12%. The CFO, Lakshmi Iyer, asks which is cheaper. The IT head expects to use the software for at least eight years. The manufacturing head points out that the industry is changing quickly and five years is all anyone can see.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the present value of cost for each option over five years.
  2. Repeat for eight years.
  3. At about what horizon are the two equal?
  4. What factors outside the arithmetic should influence the choice?
  5. If you were the vendor and preferred subscriptions, what would you offer to tip the decision?
Model answer

1. Five years, annuity factor 3.605. Perpetual: 3.0 + 0.45 × 3.605 = 3.0 + 1.62 = ₹4.62 crore. Subscription: 1.1 × 3.605 = ₹3.97 crore. Subscription is cheaper by ₹0.66 crore.

2. Eight years, annuity factor 4.968. Perpetual: 3.0 + 0.45 × 4.968 = ₹5.24 crore. Subscription: 1.1 × 4.968 = ₹5.46 crore. Perpetual is cheaper by ₹0.23 crore.

3. They are equal when 3.0 + 0.45 × A = 1.1 × A, so A = 4.62. The 12% annuity factor is 4.564 at seven years and 4.968 at eight, so break-even is a little over seven years.

4. Flexibility: a subscription can be scaled down or ended, and that option is valuable when the future is uncertain. Cash: Sahyadri's free cash flow and borrowing capacity may make ₹3 crore upfront unattractive regardless of present value. Accounting: the treatment affects EBITDA, as Month 1 showed. Price risk: subscription prices can rise at renewal, while a perpetual licence fixes the main cost. Product direction: vendors increasingly reserve new capabilities for subscription offerings.

5. Remove the customer's main risk. Offer a five-year price lock or a cap on annual increases, include capabilities the perpetual version lacks, and allow seats to flex with production volume. Each addresses a factor in question 4 without cutting the headline price.

What the case teaches. The cheaper option depends on the horizon and the discount rate, and reasonable people in the same company hold different views on both. State your assumptions, show the break-even, and then discuss what the arithmetic leaves out.

Growth questions are percentage multipliers applied repeatedly. Sequence questions are about finding the rule.

Rules

  • Simple interest = principal × rate × time.
  • Compound amount = principal × (1 + rate)ⁿ.
  • Repeated change: falling 20% a year for two years leaves 0.8 × 0.8 = 64%.
  • Arithmetic sequence: constant difference d. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d.
  • Geometric sequence: constant ratio. Doubling every period is a ratio of 2.

Net present value is the single most important decision rule in finance. It tells you how much richer a project makes the company, in today's money.

The rule

NPV = present value of future cash flows − initial investment

Accept projects with a positive NPV. Among alternatives that exclude each other, choose the highest NPV.

Worked example

Take the virtual commissioning project from Month 1: invest ₹150 lakh, receive a net ₹130 lakh at the end of each of three years. At 12%:

NPV = −150 + 130 × 2.402 = −150 + 312.2 = ₹162.2 lakh

The project creates ₹162 lakh of value. Compare that with the undiscounted surplus of 390 − 150 = ₹240 lakh. A third of the apparent gain was the time value of money.

Why NPV is the right rule

  • It uses all the cash flows, not only those before payback.
  • It accounts for timing and for risk, through the discount rate.
  • It measures value in rupees, so NPVs of independent projects can be added.
  • A positive NPV means the project earns more than investors could get elsewhere at the same risk. That is what creating value means.

Sensitivity analysis

An NPV is only as good as its inputs, so test them one at a time.

The project is robust to delay and to the discount rate, and fragile to benefit shortfall. In Month 1 the half-benefit case showed a 10% positive ROI. NPV shows it actually destroys value, because ₹165 lakh spread over three years is worth less than ₹150 lakh today.

Break-even: NPV is zero when the annual net benefit is 150 ÷ 2.402 = ₹62.4 lakh.

In your job

A project has an NPV of zero. This means it:

  1. Earns exactly the required return
  2. Makes no accounting profit
  3. Never pays back

Investors get precisely the return they could get elsewhere.

  2. Do only the first
  3. Do neither

Managers like percentages and time periods more than present values, so IRR and payback stay popular. Both are useful and both can lead you to the wrong choice.

Internal rate of return

The IRR is the discount rate at which a project's NPV is zero. It is the return the project itself earns. The rule: accept if the IRR exceeds the required rate.

Invest ₹100 lakh and receive ₹60 lakh at the end of each of two years. The IRR solves 60 ÷ (1 + r) + 60 ÷ (1 + r)² = 100, which gives about 13.1%. Against a 12% hurdle the project just passes. In a spreadsheet, use the IRR function.

Where IRR misleads

Scale. Compare two projects that exclude each other, at a 10% required rate.

ProjectInvest nowReceive in 1 yearIRRNPV at 10%
A10013030%18.2
B1,0001,20020%90.9

A has the higher IRR. B makes the company five times richer. If you can only do one, choose B. A high percentage on a small amount is worth less than a good percentage on a large one.

Timing. IRR favours projects with early cash flows even when a slower project has a higher NPV.

Odd cash flow patterns. If cash flows change sign more than once, for example a large decommissioning cost at the end, there can be several IRRs or none.

Reinvestment. IRR implicitly assumes that cash received can be reinvested at the IRR itself, which is rarely true for very high figures.

When NPV and IRR disagree, follow NPV.

Payback

Payback ignores everything after the cut-off and ignores timing within it. It favours short-lived projects. It survives because it is easy and because it is a rough measure of risk: the sooner the money is back, the less can go wrong. Discounted payback, using present values, repairs the timing flaw.

Profitability index

When capital is rationed, rank projects by NPV per rupee invested. A plant with a ₹5 crore budget and ₹12 crore of positive-NPV proposals should fund those with the highest index first.

How companies really decide

Surveys of finance chiefs consistently find most firms calculate NPV and IRR together and still look at payback. Strategic fit, the sponsor's credibility and the capital budget matter as well. The arithmetic is the entry ticket.

In your job

Software and services proposals are small next to a new press line, so their IRR is often spectacular and their NPV modest. Present both, and position the project as cheap insurance for the big capital spend it protects.

Traps to recognise

  • Out of scope. About the topic, not about the argument.
  • Opposite. Strengthens when asked to weaken.
  • Too weak. "Some" customers, when the argument needs "most".

Discounting is mechanical. The skill in capital budgeting is deciding which cash flows belong in the analysis.

Rules

  1. Cash, not profit. Start from operating profit, then add back depreciation and subtract capex and working capital changes.
  2. Incremental only. Count what changes because of the project: with it minus without it.
  3. Ignore sunk costs. Money already spent on a feasibility study is gone.
  4. Include opportunity costs. A project using an empty building the firm could rent out bears the lost rent.
  5. Include side effects. A new product that takes sales from an old one should carry that loss.
  6. After tax. Depreciation is not a cash flow, but it reduces tax, which is. This is the depreciation tax shield.
  7. Leave out financing. Interest is not deducted. The discount rate already covers the cost of money.
  8. Remember working capital. It is invested at the start and usually recovered at the end.

Worked example

Terminal value

If the asset has resale value or the project continues beyond the forecast, add a terminal value in the last year. Be conservative. Terminal values are where optimistic cases hide.

Two ways to add capacity. One has the better percentage return and the other the better value. Decide which to build.

The situation

Kaveri needs more welding capacity. Its plant head and its finance head each favour a different option. Both last five years, and Kaveri can only do one. The discount rate is 12%. Amounts are in ₹ lakh and cash flows are after tax.

OptionInvestmentCash flow per year

Your questions

  1. Calculate the NPV of each option.
  2. Analyse the extra ₹400 lakh that R needs over M as a project in itself. Is it worth spending?
  3. Recalculate both NPVs if cash flows are 20% lower.
  4. Which option carries more risk, and why?
  5. What would you recommend, and what would you want to find out first?
Model answer

1. M: −200 + 70 × 3.605 = ₹52.3 lakh. R: −600 + 190 × 3.605 = ₹84.9 lakh. R creates more value despite the lower IRR.

2. The increment is 400 more invested for 120 more a year. NPV = −400 + 120 × 3.605 = ₹32.6 lakh. Its IRR is about 15%, above the 12% hurdle. The extra money earns more than it costs, so on the base case R is right. This incremental test is the clean way to resolve a conflict between NPV and IRR.

3. M: −200 + 56 × 3.605 = ₹1.9 lakh, roughly break-even. R: −600 + 152 × 3.605 = −₹52.0 lakh. Under weak demand M survives and R destroys value.

4. R is riskier. It commits three times the capital, and its payoff depends on volume being there. This is operating leverage from Month 1 seen again in capital budgeting. The swing between base and downside is about 137 for R and 50 for M.

5. The answer depends on confidence in demand. If the probability of the low-volume case is p, R is better when 84.9 − 137p exceeds 52.3 − 50p, which is when p is below about 37%. So: recommend R if the main customer will give a volume commitment or the order book covers the forecast, and M otherwise. Find out first whether R can be staged, with one robot now and provision for a second, because that would keep most of the upside with less capital at risk. Also check what each option does to quality and delivery, since those affect future orders and are not in the cash flows.

What the case teaches. For mutually exclusive projects use NPV and test the increment. Then ask how the ranking changes under a realistic downside. The best answer is often a third option that buys flexibility.
