Investors demand a higher return for bearing risk. The surprising part is which risk they are paid for.

Measuring risk

In finance, risk is the variability of returns, usually measured by standard deviation. Over long periods shares have returned several percentage points a year more than government bonds, and their yearly returns have swung far more widely. That extra average return is the equity risk premium.

Diversification

Hold one share and you bear everything that can happen to that company. Hold thirty across different industries and much of that cancels out: one firm's strike is offset by another's good quarter. The portfolio's variability falls sharply as shares are added and then levels off.

What cancels is specific risk: events peculiar to one company. What remains is market risk: recessions, interest rates and oil prices hit almost every firm together and cannot be diversified away.

The key idea

Diversification is nearly free, so investors are not paid for risk they could have diversified away. They are paid only for market risk. A company's cost of capital therefore depends on how sensitive it is to the economy, not on how uncertain its own results are in isolation.

Beta

Beta measures that sensitivity. A beta of 1 means the share moves with the market on average. A beta of 1.5 means it tends to rise 15% when the market rises 10%, and fall 15% when the market falls 10%. A beta of 0.6 is defensive.

Typical betaKinds of businessWhy
Below 1Utilities, consumer staples, pharmaceuticalsDemand barely changes with the economy
Around 1Diversified industrialsMove with the economy
Above 1Capital goods, automotive, metals, propertyCustomers postpone purchases in a downturn; high fixed costs magnify the swing

Two things raise beta: cyclical revenue and high operating leverage. Debt raises it further, because interest is a fixed charge.

What this means for projects

A risk that is specific to the project, such as whether the new line commissions on time, belongs in the expected cash flows as a probability-weighted estimate. A risk tied to the economy, such as demand falling in a recession, belongs in the discount rate.

In your job

Machine builders and automotive suppliers are high-beta businesses, so their investors demand high returns and their hurdle rates are high. That is one reason your customers want fast payback. It is built into how their capital is priced.

Lenders state the return they want in a loan agreement. Shareholders do not, so their required return has to be estimated. The standard tool is the capital asset pricing model.

The formula

Cost of equity = Risk-free rate + Beta × Equity risk premium

  • Risk-free rate: the yield on long-term government bonds in the same currency as the cash flows.
  • Equity risk premium: the extra return investors expect from shares over government bonds. Estimates for India commonly fall between about 5% and 8%. It cannot be observed directly, and practitioners disagree.
  • Beta: estimated from the share's past movements against the market, or taken from comparable listed companies.

Worked example

Risk-free rate 7%, equity risk premium 6%, beta 1.2.

Cost of equity = 7% + 1.2 × 6% = 14.2%

Shareholders of this company expect 14.2% a year. If management invests their money in projects earning less, the share price falls.

Equity is the expensive money

Many managers think of equity as free because it carries no interest. It is the costliest capital a firm has. Shareholders are paid last and bear the most risk, so they require the most. Retained profit is equity too: keeping it in the business is only justified if it earns the cost of equity.

How good is the model?

The model is simple and its inputs are estimates. Betas move over time. Research has found that other factors, such as company size and valuation, also help explain returns. It remains the standard in corporate practice because it is transparent and nothing else is clearly better for setting hurdle rates. Treat the output as a range of about ±2 percentage points.

Unlisted companies and divisions

A private company or a division has no share price. Use the betas of listed companies in the same line of business, adjusted for differences in debt. This "pure-play" method is also how to set a hurdle rate for a project unlike the rest of the firm.

In your job

A software vendor and an automotive customer in the same deal have different costs of capital. A payment plan that suits both exists because the lower-cost party can finance the higher-cost one. Vendor financing is built on this.

Rules

  • Two overlapping groups: Total = A + B − both + neither.
  • Averages: work with sums. Sum = mean × count.
  • Median: the middle of the sorted list, or the mean of the two middle values.
  • Standard deviation: adding a constant to every value leaves it unchanged. Multiplying every value by k multiplies it by k.
  • Weighted average of group percentages: weight by each group's size.

A company is funded by a mix of debt and equity. The blended cost of that mix is the rate its average investment must beat.

The formula

WACC = (E ÷ V) × cost of equity + (D ÷ V) × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate)

E and D are the market values of equity and debt and V is their sum. Debt is multiplied by (1 − tax rate) because interest is deductible: each rupee of interest saves 25 paise of tax at a 25% rate.

Worked example

A firm is funded 60% by equity costing 14.2% and 40% by debt costing 9% before tax. Tax is 25%.

WACC = 0.6 × 14.2% + 0.4 × 9% × 0.75 = 8.52% + 2.70% = 11.2%

On average, this firm's investments must return more than 11.2% to create value.

Points that are often missed

  • Use market values. Book equity understates the weight of equity for most profitable firms.
  • Use target proportions, not this year's accident.
  • Debt looks cheap but has limits. Adding debt raises the risk and cost of both the remaining equity and the debt. WACC does not keep falling as debt rises.
  • WACC is for projects of average risk for the firm. A project that is riskier or safer than the company as a whole needs a different rate.

From WACC to hurdle rate

Many companies set the hurdle rate above WACC, often by several points. Their reasons: project forecasts tend to be optimistic, management attention is limited, and some approved projects will produce nothing. The practice is common and arguable. A high single hurdle rejects safe, sound projects and still lets through risky ones with inflated forecasts.

ROCE and WACC

Month 1 said ROCE should exceed the cost of capital. Now the comparison is concrete. Kaveri's ROCE was 19.2% before tax, or about 14.4% after 25% tax. Against a WACC near 12%, it creates value. The gap, multiplied by capital employed, is called economic profit or economic value added: (14.4% − 12%) × 78 = about ₹1.9 crore a year.

In your job

If a customer says the hurdle rate is 18%, that is policy and you cannot argue with it. You can show that your project's risk is low: benefits measured on existing volume, proven at a reference site, staged with a pilot. Low risk is what persuades a committee to approve near the hurdle.

The situation

Deccan Driveline wants to build a line for electric vehicle drive-unit housings. The investment is ₹300 crore and the forecast is an after-tax cash flow of ₹75 crore a year for six years. The technology and customers are new to Deccan.

Finance provides these figures. Market value of equity is ₹1,800 crore and debt is ₹600 crore. Beta is 1.1, the risk-free rate is 7% and the equity risk premium is 6%. Debt costs 9% before tax and the tax rate is 25%.

The CFO suggests using the company WACC. The head of strategy argues that an unproven business deserves 15%. Annuity factors for six years are 4.111 at 12% and 3.784 at 15%.

Your questions

  1. Calculate Deccan's cost of equity and WACC.
  2. Calculate the project's NPV at 12% and at 15%.
  3. Estimate the project's IRR.
  4. Which rate is appropriate, and why?
  5. What would you advise the board?
Model answer

1. Cost of equity = 7 + 1.1 × 6 = 13.6%. Weights are 1,800 ÷ 2,400 = 75% equity and 25% debt. WACC = 0.75 × 13.6 + 0.25 × 9 × 0.75 = 10.2 + 1.7 = 11.9%, or about 12%.

2. At 12%: −300 + 75 × 4.111 = +₹8.3 crore. At 15%: −300 + 75 × 3.784 = −₹16.2 crore.

3. The IRR is where the annuity factor equals 300 ÷ 75 = 4.0. That lies between the two given rates, at about 13%.

4. The company WACC reflects the risk of Deccan's existing business: known customers, proven processes. The EV line has less certain volumes, an unproven customer base and technology risk. A rate above 12% is justified in principle. But most of those risks are specific to the project, and the better treatment is to put them in the cash flows as scenarios and keep a moderate premium for the extra market sensitivity of a young industry. A flat 15% is a blunt substitute for doing that work.

5. An IRR of 13% against a WACC of 12% is a thin margin on a ₹300 crore bet. The base-case forecast must not be a best case. Advise the board to ask for three scenarios with probabilities, to seek a volume commitment or co-investment from the launch customer, and to look for a staged design that defers part of the capital until orders are firm. If the project is strategically necessary because combustion driveline volumes will decline, that argument should be made explicitly with the cost of not investing, and not hidden in a flattering forecast.

What the case teaches. When a decision flips with a three-point change in discount rate, the project is marginal. Further debate about the rate adds little. The useful work is improving the cash flows, reducing the capital at risk, or being honest that the case is strategic.

A business passage in the style the exam favours. Give yourself eight minutes.

Finance textbooks instruct managers to accept any investment whose expected return exceeds the firm's cost of capital. Surveys of large companies have repeatedly found that practice departs from this advice. The hurdle rates firms report using are typically several percentage points above any plausible estimate of their cost of capital, and they change little when interest rates move.

One explanation holds that managers distrust the forecasts they are shown. Project sponsors have every incentive to be optimistic, and a high hurdle is a crude correction for bias that senior managers cannot identify project by project. A second explanation points to constraints other than money. A firm may have the funds for twenty projects but the experienced managers to run only eight. The hurdle rate then rations management attention, and the price of that scarce resource is hidden inside the required return.

Both explanations imply that a high hurdle may be a sensible response to real conditions. Both also have a cost. A uniform premium penalises projects whose forecasts are reliable, such as cost reductions on existing lines, as heavily as speculative ventures, and so steers firms away from precisely the investments they can evaluate best.

The passage is primarily concerned with:

  1. Showing that finance textbooks are wrong
  2. Explaining why firms set hurdle rates above their cost of capital and noting a drawback
  3. Arguing that interest rates do not matter
  4. Describing how to forecast project returns

A puzzle, two explanations, then a cost.

According to the second explanation, a high hurdle rate serves to:

  1. Correct optimistic forecasts
  2. Reduce borrowing
  3. Reward shareholders
  4. Allocate limited managerial capacity

Paragraph 2 says it rations management attention.

The author would most likely agree that:

  1. A single high hurdle rate can cause a firm to reject sound low-risk projects
  2. Firms should never exceed their cost of capital in setting hurdles
  3. Project sponsors are usually pessimistic
  4. Speculative ventures should use lower rates

The final paragraph states this cost directly.

The statement that hurdle rates "change little when interest rates move" is included to:

  1. Show that managers ignore finance entirely
  2. Prove that interest rates are stable
  3. Support the claim that hurdle rates are not simply derived from the cost of capital
  4. Suggest that surveys are unreliable

If hurdles tracked the cost of capital they would move with interest rates.

A company is worth the present value of the cash it will generate. Valuation applies NPV to a whole business.

Free cash flow

The cash available to all investors after the business has paid its costs, taxes and investment needs:

Free cash flow = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + depreciation − capex − increase in working capital

It is calculated before interest, because it belongs to lenders and shareholders together, and is discounted at WACC.

Two stages

  1. Explicit forecast: five to ten years of free cash flow, year by year.
  2. Terminal value: everything after that, usually as a growing perpetuity.

Terminal value = next year's free cash flow ÷ (WACC − long-term growth)

Long-term growth must be modest. No company can grow faster than the economy for ever, so 4 to 6% in nominal rupee terms is a typical range.

From enterprise value to equity value

The present value of free cash flows is the enterprise value: the value of the operations to all investors.

Equity value = Enterprise value − debt + cash

Debt minus cash is called net debt.

Worked example

A stable business will produce free cash flow of ₹100 crore next year, growing 5% a year. WACC is 11%. Enterprise value = 100 ÷ (0.11 − 0.05) = ₹1,667 crore. It has debt of ₹300 crore and cash of ₹50 crore, so equity is worth 1,667 − 250 = ₹1,417 crore. With 10 crore shares, that is about ₹142 a share.

What drives value

Three things: how much cash the business generates, how fast that grows, and how risky it is. Growth adds value only when the return on new investment exceeds the cost of capital. Growth that earns less than WACC destroys value, however impressive the revenue line looks.

Kaveri on multiples

Suppose comparable listed capital-goods firms trade at about 8 times EBITDA. Kaveri's EBITDA is ₹20 crore, so its enterprise value is about ₹160 crore. Net debt is 30 − 4 = ₹26 crore, so equity is worth about ₹134 crore. Against profit after tax of ₹9 crore, that implies a P/E of about 15.

Why multiples differ

A multiple is high when investors expect high growth, high returns on capital, or low risk. Software firms often trade at 20 to 40 times EBITDA and forging companies at 6 to 10, because software has higher margins, lower capital needs, recurring revenue and faster growth. The growing perpetuity formula shows the link: value ÷ cash flow = 1 ÷ (r − g). A small gap between r and g gives a large multiple.

Recurring revenue

Investors pay far more for revenue that repeats without being re-sold each year. This is the financial reason software vendors have moved to subscriptions despite the short-term revenue dip you saw in Month 1. It is also why machine builders want service contracts and why "as a service" models keep spreading.

Using multiples carefully

  • Choose comparable firms that really are comparable in growth, margin and risk.
  • Use the same definition of earnings for every company.
  • Remember that if the whole sector is overpriced, multiples will make your target look reasonable.
  • Cross-check with a discounted cash flow. When the two disagree, understand why.

The trade-off

Debt has a tax advantage and is cheaper. It also brings the risk of financial distress: a company that cannot pay interest can be taken over by its lenders. Long before that, a heavily indebted firm loses customers, suppliers tighten terms and good people leave.

The right level of debt balances the tax saving against the expected cost of distress. Firms with stable cash flows and solid assets, such as utilities, can carry a lot. Firms with volatile cash flows or mainly intangible assets, such as software companies and cyclical capital-goods makers, should carry little.

Leverage and return on equity

Debt magnifies the return to shareholders in both directions. A business worth ₹100 crore earns ₹15 crore before interest. Funded entirely by equity, the return is 15%. Funded with ₹60 crore of debt at 9%, interest is ₹5.4 crore and the return on ₹40 crore of equity is 9.6 ÷ 40 = 24%. If earnings halve to ₹7.5 crore, the all-equity return is 7.5% and the leveraged return is 2.1 ÷ 40 = 5.3%. Taxes are ignored here for clarity.

How a leveraged buyout makes money

A private equity firm buys a company using a large share of debt, improves it, and sells after four to seven years.

Example: buy a business with EBITDA of ₹20 crore at 8 times, for ₹160 crore, using ₹96 crore of debt and ₹64 crore of equity. Over five years, grow EBITDA to ₹30 crore and use the company's cash to repay debt down to ₹56 crore. Sell at the same 8 times: ₹240 crore. Equity receives 240 − 56 = ₹184 crore, which is 2.9 times the ₹64 crore invested, about 23.5% a year.

The return has three sources: EBITDA growth, debt repayment, and any rise in the multiple. Because the first two depend on cash generation, companies owned this way focus hard on EBITDA, working capital and capex discipline.

Paying shareholders

A company returns cash through dividends or by buying back its own shares. The principle is the one from capital budgeting: if the firm has no projects earning above its cost of capital, it should give the money back.

It may also have a lower cost of capital. Those gains belong partly to the seller in negotiation. Advice: the offer is fair to generous on a standalone basis. Before accepting, Meera should invite one or two other bidders to test the price, since a second bidder is the most reliable way to capture part of the synergy. She should also examine the terms: how much is paid in cash at closing, how much depends on future targets, and what happens to employees and to her own role. And she should compare the offer with what she believes Kaveri is worth under her own plan, including the risk of carrying it out.

What the case teaches. Use two methods and compare. Value to a specific buyer can exceed standalone value, and competition between buyers decides who keeps the difference. Terms can matter as much as price.
