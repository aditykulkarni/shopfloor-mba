Engineers often think marketing means brochures. In a business school it means deciding whom to serve, with what, at what price and through which route. Finance evaluates the plan that marketing creates.

The central idea

Customers do not buy products. They buy solutions to problems, and they compare your solution with their next best alternative. Marketing strategy rests on three decisions, usually abbreviated STP:

  1. Segmentation: divide the market into groups that want different things.
  2. Targeting: choose which groups to serve.
  3. Positioning: decide what you want those groups to believe about you.

Product, price, channel and promotion follow from these.

Why segment

A company that tries to serve everyone designs for an average customer who does not exist. Resources are limited, and customers differ in what they value and in what they cost to serve.

Ways to segment industrial markets

BasisExamplesUsefulness
FirmographicIndustry, size, region, ownershipEasy to find and count. Weak predictor of needs.
OperatingProduction type, technology in use, installed systemsGood for technical fit
PurchasingCentral or plant-level buying, tender or relationshipShapes the sales approach
NeedsWhat problem is urgent: launch speed, cost, quality, complianceBest predictor of what they will pay for. Harder to observe.
BehaviourLoyalty, usage intensity, willingness to adopt new methodsGood for prioritising accounts

The practical method is to segment by needs and then find firmographic markers that let sales teams spot each group. "Tier-1 suppliers launching more than two new lines a year" is a needs segment described in observable terms.

What makes a segment useful

  • Measurable: you can count it.
  • Substantial: large enough to be worth a tailored offer.
  • Accessible: you can reach it through some channel.
  • Different: it responds differently from other segments.
  • Actionable: you can actually build something for it.

Example

Buyers of welding fixtures could be split by industry. A more useful split is by need. Some want the lowest price for a standard part. Some want a line running by a fixed start-of-production date and will pay for certainty. Some want a partner who designs the whole cell. Each group needs a different offer, price and salesperson, even inside the same industry.

In your job

The Dassault industry names you work under are firmographic segments. Inside each one, the accounts that buy fastest share a need, such as a new plant, a new product platform or a compliance deadline. Spotting the need marker is better territory planning than sorting by company size.

The order of the three core marketing decisions is:

  1. Positioning, targeting, segmentation
  2. Segmentation, targeting, positioning
  3. Targeting, positioning, segmentation

Divide, choose, then decide what to stand for.

Choosing a target means choosing whom not to serve. Positioning means choosing the one or two things you want to be known for, and accepting that you will not be known for the rest.

Choosing targets

Judge each segment on two dimensions:

  • Attractiveness: size, growth, profitability, intensity of competition.
  • Fit: can you serve it better than rivals with the capabilities you have?

A large, fast-growing segment in which you have no advantage is a trap. A small segment in which you are clearly the best choice is often the better place to start.

Positioning

A position is the place your offer holds in the customer's mind relative to alternatives. A working template:

For [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [main alternative], it [point of difference].

Example for Kaveri: "For tier-1 suppliers with a fixed start-of-production date, Kaveri is a welding-cell builder that commissions on time, first time. Unlike low-cost fixture shops, it validates every cell virtually before shipping."

The parts of a position

  • Competitive alternatives: what the customer would do without you, including doing nothing or doing it in-house.
  • Points of parity: what you must match to be considered at all.
  • Points of difference: what you have that alternatives lack.
  • Value: what those differences let the customer achieve.
  • Proof: references, data and demonstrations that make the claim believable.

Common failures

  • Feature lists. Describing what the product does and leaving the customer to work out why it matters.
  • Everything for everyone. A position that excludes nobody says nothing.
  • Claims without proof. Every vendor says "end-to-end" and "seamless".
  • The wrong comparison. If customers compare you with spreadsheets and you argue against a rival product, you answer a question they did not ask.

The value proposition

A value proposition states the benefits a specific customer gets, compared with the next best alternative, in terms they can verify. The strongest kind names the one or two differences that matter most to that customer and puts a number on them.

In your job

A demo is positioning in action. Before building one, write the positioning sentence for that account: who is in the room, what they would otherwise do, and the single difference you want them to remember. Then cut every screen that does not serve it.

Passage (aim for 90 seconds)

Firms that sell to other firms have long been advised to listen to their customers. The advice is sound for improving existing products. It can mislead when a market is about to change. Established customers ask for more of what they already use: higher capacity, greater precision, additional features. A supplier that follows them faithfully will keep refining its current product while a cheaper and initially inferior alternative gains a foothold among buyers the supplier considers unimportant. By the time that alternative is good enough for mainstream customers, the supplier has neither the cost structure nor the experience to compete with it. The lesson is not that customers should be ignored, but that the customers who matter most today are poor guides to the products that will matter tomorrow.

The main point of the passage is that:

  1. Suppliers should stop listening to customers
  2. Cheaper products are always better
  3. Following current customers closely can leave a firm unprepared for a shift in its market
  4. Established customers want fewer features

The last sentence states it and rules out option (a).

According to the passage, the alternative product first succeeds with:

  1. Buyers the established supplier regards as unimportant
  2. The supplier's largest customers
  3. Government purchasers
  4. Buyers who want greater precision

Stated directly.

The author would most likely recommend that an established supplier:

  1. Lower its prices immediately
  2. Stop adding features
  3. Survey its largest customers more often
  4. Pay attention to low-end buyers and to products it currently considers inferior

That is where the passage says the threat begins.

Two skills every strategy or product role uses constantly: finding out what customers really need, and estimating how big an opportunity is.

Jobs to be done

Customers "hire" a product to do a job. Asking what job they are hiring it for often reveals competitors and needs that a feature comparison hides. A plant buying simulation software is hiring it to avoid an expensive surprise during launch. The alternatives for that job include an experienced integrator, extra buffer time in the schedule, and physical mock-ups, as well as rival software.

Interviewing customers

People are polite and poor at predicting their own behaviour. Rules that get honest information:

  • Ask about the past, not the future. "Tell me about the last line you launched" beats "Would you use a tool that…?"
  • Ask what they do today and what it costs them.
  • Ask what they have already tried. A problem nobody has tried to solve is rarely painful enough to pay for.
  • Do not pitch during the interview. Once you pitch, you get compliments instead of facts.

Market sizing

Three nested numbers:

  • TAM, total addressable market: annual spend if every possible customer bought.
  • SAM, serviceable market: the part your product and channels can actually serve.
  • SOM, obtainable market: the share you can realistically win in a few years.

Worked example

The numbers here are invented to show the method. Suppose a commissioning-simulation service is aimed at Indian plants with robotic welding or assembly lines.

StepAssumptionResult
Plants with robotic linesEstimate from industry directories1,500
Average annual spend per plant₹40 lakhTAM ₹600 crore
Plants with in-house engineering able to adopt it40%SAM ₹240 crore
Share winnable in three years10%SOM ₹24 crore

This is a bottom-up estimate: count customers and multiply by spend. A top-down estimate starts from a published industry total and takes percentages. Bottom-up is more credible because every assumption can be checked. Do both and see whether they agree within a factor of two.

Present the assumptions more prominently than the answer. A reviewer who can see them will argue about one step. A reviewer who cannot will reject the whole figure.

In your job

Requirement workshops are customer research. Use the same rules: ask how the work was done on the last programme, what went wrong and what it cost, before discussing any capability. The answers give you the baseline for a business case.

Which interview question gives the most reliable information?

  1. "Would you buy this if we built it?"
  2. "How did you handle this the last time it happened?"
  3. "Do you think this is a good idea?"

Past behaviour is fact. Predictions and opinions are not.

A company does not make a purchase. A group of people with different interests does, over months. Understanding that group is the core of selling to industry.

How business buying differs

  • Fewer, larger customers.
  • Several people involved in each decision.
  • Formal processes: specifications, tenders, approvals.
  • Demand derived from the customer's own sales.
  • Long relationships after the sale.

The buying centre

RoleWho it often isWhat they care about
InitiatorAn engineer or manager with a problemGetting the problem acknowledged
UserEngineers, planners, operatorsEase of use, effect on daily work
InfluencerIT, quality, consultantsStandards, integration, risk
Economic buyerPlant head, business unit head, CFOReturn, budget, priorities
PurchaserProcurementPrice, terms, compliance
GatekeeperAssistants, IT security, procurementControls access and information

One person can hold several roles. The common mistake is to sell to the user, who likes the product, and never reach the economic buyer, who has to choose between it and three other requests for the same money.

Stages of a purchase

  1. A problem is recognised.
  2. The need is defined and a specification written.
  3. Suppliers are searched for and shortlisted.
  4. Proposals are requested and evaluated.
  5. A supplier is selected and terms negotiated.
  6. Delivery is reviewed and the relationship continues or ends.

Whoever shapes stage 2 usually wins. A supplier who first appears when the request for proposal arrives is competing on someone else's specification.

Three buying situations

  • Straight rebuy: routine reorder. The incumbent wins unless it fails.
  • Modified rebuy: the customer wants better terms or performance. The incumbent is exposed.
  • New task: a first-time purchase. Risk is high, the buying group is large, and the supplier who teaches the customer how to buy has the advantage.

Risk dominates

For the people involved, a bad purchase can damage a career, while a good one is soon forgotten. They reduce personal risk by choosing known vendors, asking for references and spreading the decision across a committee. A proposal should lower perceived risk as deliberately as it raises perceived value.

In your job

For every opportunity, write the names against the six roles. A blank next to "economic buyer" means the deal is not yet real, however good the workshops felt.

There are three ways to set a price: from your costs, from competitors' prices, or from the value to the customer. Only the third captures what a differentiated product is worth.

Three approaches

ApproachLogicProblem
Cost-plusCost plus a standard marginIgnores what customers will pay. Underprices strong products and overprices weak ones.
Competition-basedMatch or undercut rivalsTreats your product as a commodity.
Value-basedA share of the measurable benefit to the customerNeeds real knowledge of the customer's economics.

Cost sets the floor. Value sets the ceiling. Competition decides where between them you can land.

Economic value to the customer

Economic value = price of the next best alternative + value of your differences − customer's switching and adoption costs

Example: a customer's alternative costs ₹50 lakh. Over the same period your solution saves a further ₹30 lakh in rework and launch delay. Adopting it costs the customer ₹5 lakh in training and migration.

Economic value = 50 + 30 − 5 = ₹75 lakh

At any price below ₹75 lakh the customer is better off with you. At ₹62 lakh you keep ₹12 lakh of the extra value and the customer keeps ₹13 lakh. Sharing matters. A customer who gets nothing extra has no reason to take the risk of switching.

Making it work

  1. Identify the real alternative for this segment.
  2. Quantify each difference in the customer's own numbers. This is the business-case skill from Months 1 and 3.
  3. Discount for doubt. Customers believe a fraction of claimed value. Proof raises that fraction.
  4. Choose a price metric that scales with value: per user, per line, per unit produced, per transaction.
  5. Design a price structure with tiers, so each segment pays in proportion to what it gains.

The price metric matters

A simulation tool priced per named user earns the same from a plant launching one line as from one launching six. Priced per line or per project, revenue follows value. Changing the metric is often worth more than changing the price level.

Value has to be communicated

A price justified by a calculation the buyer has never seen is only a high price. Value selling means building the calculation with the customer, using their inputs, so that they own the result.

In your job

Pre-sales is where value gets quantified or does not. When the customer's own engineers supply the baseline numbers during a workshop, the business case carries their authority inside their company. That is worth more than any benchmark you bring.

A price is set once and given away a hundred times, one deal at a time. Most of the profit in pricing is won or lost in discounting.

What a discount costs

Month 1 showed that a 5% price cut can halve profit. The same arithmetic applies to every deal. The extra volume needed to make up for a discount is:

Required volume increase = discount ÷ (contribution margin − discount)

Contribution margin5% discount10% discount20% discount
30%+20%+50%+200%
40%+14%+33%+100%
80% (software)+7%+14%+33%

High-margin businesses can afford discounts in the short run, and that is why software sales teams discount freely. The longer-term damage is to price expectations: this year's discount becomes next renewal's starting point.

The price waterfall

Between list price and the money a company really keeps lie many leaks: negotiated discounts, volume rebates, free services, extended payment terms, free freight, special support. The amount left is the pocket price. Companies that measure it usually find large and unexplained differences between similar customers, and that some of their biggest accounts are their least profitable.

Give and get

The discipline that protects price: never concede without receiving something. Trade a lower price for a longer term, a larger scope, faster payment, a reference, or fewer services. Remove value when you remove price, so that the price of the original offer stays intact.

Why discounts happen

  • Sales people paid on revenue will trade margin for volume.
  • Quarter-end pressure gives buyers a calendar to exploit.
  • Without quantified value there is nothing to defend the price with.
  • Procurement is trained to ask and measured on what it gets.

Price, volume and mix

When revenue changes, split it into three parts: price (the same things at different prices), volume (more or fewer units) and mix (a different blend of products). A company can report rising revenue while its prices erode, if volume and mix hide it. Always ask for the split.

In your job

Technical consultants often give away the most expensive discount without noticing: unpriced effort. Extra workshops, proof-of-concept builds and custom demos are part of the waterfall. Scope them deliberately and tie each to a customer commitment.

A new product, three ways to price it and two kinds of customer. Design the price.

The situation

Meridian is launching a virtual commissioning module. Three proposals are on the table for its annual subscription price.

  • Finance: cost to deliver and support is about ₹8 lakh a customer a year. Add 50% and charge ₹12 lakh.
  • Sales: the main competitor charges ₹35 lakh. Charge ₹30 lakh and win on price.
  • Product: price on value.

Research with pilot customers shows two groups. Large tier-1 suppliers launching several lines a year gain about ₹130 lakh a year. Smaller firms launching one line every year or two gain about ₹40 lakh. Customers typically believe about half of a vendor's value claim before they have proof. Adoption costs each customer about ₹10 lakh in the first year.

Your questions

  1. What is wrong with the cost-plus price?
  2. What is the risk in the competitor-minus price?
  3. Estimate what each group would be willing to pay in the first year.
  4. Propose a price metric and structure that serves both groups.
  5. How would you raise the share of value customers believe?
Model answer

1. At ₹12 lakh, Meridian captures under a tenth of the value created for a large customer. It also signals a minor product and sets an anchor that will be very hard to raise later.

2. Pricing just under a rival invites a response, as the game theory week showed, and frames the product as interchangeable. It also ignores the fact that ₹30 lakh is too high for small customers and too low for large ones.

3. Large: believed value is about half of 130, so ₹65 lakh, less ₹10 lakh adoption cost, gives roughly ₹55 lakh as the most they would pay in year one. They also need a share of the gain, so a price around ₹35 to 40 lakh is defensible. Small: half of 40 is ₹20 lakh, less ₹10 lakh, leaves ₹10 lakh at most. A price of ₹30 lakh would exclude them entirely.

4. Price per production line commissioned, or in tiers by number of lines, so that revenue follows use. For example: an entry tier at ₹8 to 10 lakh for one line a year with standard libraries, and an enterprise tier at ₹35 to 40 lakh for unlimited lines with advanced capabilities and support. The entry tier roughly covers cost and captures customers who will grow. The enterprise tier earns the margin. The tiers must differ in ways large customers care about, so they do not trade down.

5. Publish reference results with named customers. Offer a paid pilot on one line with success measures agreed in advance. Build the value calculation with the customer's own launch data. Consider tying part of the first-year fee to an agreed outcome, which shows confidence and moves risk away from the buyer.

What the case teaches. A single price for customers who gain very different amounts leaves money behind at the top and shuts out customers at the bottom. Segment first, choose the metric, then set the levels.

Inference questions give you facts and ask what must follow. The right answer is often dull, because it claims very little. The wrong answers are attractive because they claim more than the facts support.

Rules

  • Treat every statement as true.
  • The answer must be guaranteed by the statements, not merely likely.
  • Prefer cautious wording: "some", "at least one", "not all".
  • Reject answers that bring in outside knowledge or predict the future.

Every plant that received the new training reduced its defect rate. Plant K did not reduce its defect rate. Which must be true?

  1. Plant K's defect rate rose
  2. The training is ineffective
  3. Plant K did not receive the new training
  4. Plant K will receive the training next year

If it had received the training it would have reduced defects. Its rate might have stayed the same, so (a) is not certain.

  2. It cut prices
  3. It hired too many people

Profit is revenue minus cost. If revenue rose and profit fell, costs rose by more.
