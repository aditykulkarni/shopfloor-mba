Statistics begins with summarising many numbers in a few. The choice of summary can inform or mislead, and managers are shown misleading summaries every week.

Centre

  • Mean: the sum divided by the count. Sensitive to extreme values.
  • Median: the middle value when sorted. Not affected by extremes.
  • Mode: the most frequent value.

Seven cycle times in seconds: 48, 49, 50, 50, 51, 52, 90. The mean is 55.7. The median is 50. One stoppage dragged the mean up by almost six seconds. If you report only the mean, the station looks slow. If you report only the median, you hide the stoppage. Report both, and look at the raw data.

Spread

  • Range: highest minus lowest.
  • Variance: the average squared distance from the mean.
  • Standard deviation (SD): the square root of the variance, in the same units as the data.

For 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 the mean is 50. The squared distances are 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, which average 2. The variance is 2 and the SD is about 1.4 seconds. When working from a sample, statisticians divide by n − 1 instead of n, which here gives a variance of 2.5 and an SD of about 1.6.

The coefficient of variation is SD ÷ mean. It lets you compare variability between processes of different size, and it will matter a great deal in Month 5.

Shape

A symmetric distribution has mean and median close together. A right-skewed one has a long tail of high values and a mean above the median. Repair times, project overruns, incomes and deal sizes are all right-skewed. For skewed data, the median and percentiles describe the typical case better than the mean.

Percentiles say what share of values fall below a point. The 90th percentile lead time is the time within which 90% of orders are delivered. Customers experience the percentiles, not the average.

Three questions to ask of any average

  1. Mean or median?
  2. How spread out are the values?
  3. What was left out, and over what period?

In your job

Simulation outputs are distributions. Presenting "average throughput 58 units per hour" hides the hours at 40. Show the range or the 10th percentile, because the plant has to commit to deliveries in the bad hours too.

Probability is the arithmetic of uncertainty. Most errors people make with it come from one source: confusing the chance of A given B with the chance of B given A.

Basic rules

  • Probabilities run from 0 to 1 and all possible outcomes sum to 1.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). "At least one" problems are easiest this way.
  • And, for independent events: multiply. Two machines each up 90% of the time are both up 0.9 × 0.9 = 81% of the time.
  • Or, for events that cannot both happen: add.

Series reliability

A line has ten stations in series, each available 98% of the time, failing independently. The line runs only when all ten run: 0.98¹⁰ = 82%. Small losses multiply. This is why long automated lines need buffers or very reliable stations.

Conditional probability

P(A | B) means the probability of A given that B has happened. It is usually very different from P(B | A).

Worked example: an inspection station

2% of parts are defective. A vision system flags 95% of defective parts. It also wrongly flags 5% of good parts. A part is flagged. What is the chance it is really defective?

Take 10,000 parts. 200 are defective and 190 of them are flagged. 9,800 are good and 490 of them are flagged. In total 680 parts are flagged, of which 190 are defective: 28%.

The system is "95% accurate", yet nearly three out of four flags are false alarms. The reason is the base rate: good parts vastly outnumber bad ones, so even a small false-alarm rate produces many false flags. This reasoning is Bayes' theorem, and counting cases as above is the most reliable way to apply it.

Where this matters in management

  • A hiring test, a fraud alert, a predictive-maintenance alarm and a sales-lead score all behave like the inspection station.
  • When the thing you are looking for is rare, most alerts will be false unless the false-alarm rate is extremely low.
  • Always ask for the base rate.

In your job

Predictive maintenance and AI quality inspection are sold on detection accuracy. The customer's real experience depends on false alarms per shift. Ask for both numbers and work out the flagged-and-real percentage before a pilot.

Counting questions come down to one decision: does order matter?

Rules

  • Multiplication principle: if one choice can be made in m ways and the next in n ways, together there are m × n.
  • Arrangements of n distinct items: n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 1.
  • Combinations (order does not matter): choosing r from n is n! ÷ (r! × (n − r)!). Choosing 2 from 5 is (5 × 4) ÷ 2 = 10.
  • Probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total equally likely outcomes.
  • For "at least one", calculate 1 minus the probability of none.

Managers rarely know outcomes in advance. They can still decide well by weighing each outcome by its probability and by understanding how results spread.

Expected value

Expected value = sum of (probability × outcome)

Preparing a bid costs ₹10 lakh. The chance of winning is 30% and a win earns ₹80 lakh profit. Expected value = 0.3 × 80 − 10 = ₹14 lakh. Bid. Over many such bids, the average gain per bid approaches ₹14 lakh, even though any single bid either loses 10 or gains 70.

Expected value suits decisions that are repeated and small relative to the firm. For a single decision large enough to threaten the company, the downside matters more than the average.

Decision trees

A decision tree lays out choices and chance events in sequence. Squares are decisions and circles are chance events. Solve it from right to left: calculate the expected value at each chance node, then pick the best branch at each decision node.

Trees are most valuable for showing the worth of options: the option to stop after a pilot, to expand after early success, or to wait for information. The case in the next session works one through.

The normal distribution

Many measurements cluster in a bell shape around their mean. For a normal distribution:

  • about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean
  • about 95% lie within 2 SD
  • about 99.7% lie within 3 SD

A station has a mean cycle time of 50 seconds and an SD of 2. About 95% of cycles fall between 46 and 54 seconds. A cycle of 58 seconds is 4 SD out and signals that something unusual happened.

A z-score states how many SDs a value is from the mean: z = (value − mean) ÷ SD.

A warning about tails

Not everything is normal. Financial losses, project delays and supply disruptions have fat tails: extreme events happen far more often than a bell curve predicts. Using normal assumptions where they do not hold has ruined many risk models.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the expected net value of each option.
  2. What is the worst outcome under each?
  3. What is the pilot's information worth, and is it worth its cost?
  4. Which option would you recommend to a cautious CFO, and what could change your answer?
Model answer

1. Option A: 0.6 × 390 + 0.4 × 90 − 150 = 234 + 36 − 150 = 120. Option B: success branch 390 − 150 − 30 = 210; failure branch −30. Expected value = 0.6 × 210 + 0.4 × (−30) = 126 − 12 = 114.

2. Option A's worst case is 90 − 150 = −60. Option B's worst case is −30.

3. Without a pilot, the failure branch loses 60. With perfect information Sahyadri would avoid investing in that case and lose nothing, so the information is worth 0.4 × 60 = 24. The pilot costs 30. On expected value alone it is not quite worth buying, which is why Option A scores 120 against 114.

4. The gap is small, 6 out of about 120, and well inside the uncertainty of Farhan's own estimates. The pilot halves the worst-case loss and gives evidence that makes the second plant easier to approve, so a cautious CFO can reasonably prefer it. The answer tips firmly towards the pilot if the probability of failure is higher than 40%, if the failure case is worse than assumed, or if the pilot is cheaper. It tips towards full rollout if the pilot would delay benefits by a launch cycle, since each launch missed forfeits roughly 60 to 75 of benefit.

What the case teaches. A pilot is a purchase of information and has a price. It is worth buying when the decision would change depending on what it shows and when being wrong is expensive. Calculating that value turns "let us do a pilot to be safe" into a decision.

During the Second World War, analysts studying bombers that returned from missions recorded where each aircraft had been hit. The damage was concentrated on the wings and fuselage, and the initial recommendation was to add armour to those areas. The statistician Abraham Wald, working with a research group that advised the military, argued for the opposite. The sample, he pointed out, consisted only of aircraft that had come back. Holes in the wings showed where a bomber could be hit and still fly. The areas with little recorded damage, around the engines, were more plausibly the places where a hit brought the aircraft down, which was why so few examples of such damage appeared in the data.

The error Wald identified is now called survivorship bias. It arises whenever a selection process removes some cases before they can be observed, and the remaining cases are then treated as if they represented the whole. Studies of successful companies are especially vulnerable. A list of traits shared by firms that have prospered for decades says little unless the same traits are shown to be rarer among firms that failed, and failed firms seldom leave records as complete as those of survivors.

None of this means that data from survivors is worthless. It means that the analyst must first ask how the data came to exist.

You almost never see the whole population. You see a sample and must say how far the truth could be from what you measured.

Sampling

A sample is useful only if it is representative. Random selection is the standard protection against bias. A large biased sample is worse than a small random one, because size gives false confidence.

The standard error

If you took many samples, their means would vary. The standard error (SE) measures that variation:

SE = SD ÷ √n

The central limit theorem says that for reasonably large samples, sample means follow a normal distribution around the true mean, whatever the shape of the underlying data. This single result makes most of practical statistics possible.

Confidence intervals

95% confidence interval = sample mean ± 1.96 × SE

You time 100 cycles and get a mean of 50.2 seconds with an SD of 2.0. SE = 2.0 ÷ √100 = 0.2. The 95% interval is 50.2 ± 0.39, or about 49.8 to 50.6 seconds. If you repeated the study many times, about 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true mean.

Sample size

Because of the square root, precision is expensive. To halve the margin of error you need four times the sample. Going from 100 to 400 cycles narrows the interval from ±0.39 to ±0.20. Going from 400 to 1,600 narrows it to ±0.10. At some point more data is not worth the cost.

The margin depends on the sample size, not on the population size. A sample of 1,000 gives about the same precision for a city or a country.

Proportions

For a percentage, SE = √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). If 40 of 400 inspected parts are defective, p = 0.10 and SE = √(0.09 ÷ 400) = 0.015. The 95% interval is 10% ± 2.9%, or roughly 7% to 13%.

Someone says the new process is faster. A hypothesis test asks whether the evidence is strong enough to rule out chance.

The logic

  1. Start by assuming nothing has changed. This is the null hypothesis.
  2. Measure how far the sample result is from what the null predicts, in standard errors.
  3. Find the p-value: the probability of a result at least this extreme if the null were true.
  4. If the p-value is below a chosen threshold, usually 5%, reject the null.

Worked example

The old process averages 50.0 seconds. After a change, 64 timed cycles average 49.4 seconds with an SD of 2.4. SE = 2.4 ÷ 8 = 0.3. The result is (49.4 − 50.0) ÷ 0.3 = −2.0 standard errors from the old mean. A gap of 2 SE or more in either direction happens by chance about 4.6% of the time. The p-value is below 5%, so the improvement is statistically significant.

Two kinds of error

Really no effectReally an effect
Test says "effect"Type I error (false alarm)Correct
Test says "no effect"CorrectType II error (missed effect)

Lowering the threshold reduces false alarms and increases missed effects. Small samples miss real effects often; that is low statistical power.

What significance does not mean

  • It does not mean important. With a huge sample, a trivial 0.05-second improvement can be significant. Always ask for the size of the effect and its confidence interval.
  • It does not mean the effect is certainly real. One in twenty tests of a true null will pass at the 5% level.
  • "Not significant" does not mean "no effect". It may mean the sample was too small.
  • Many tests produce false positives. Test twenty ideas and one will look significant by chance. This is called p-hacking when done deliberately.

A/B tests

The cleanest evidence comes from randomly assigning units to an old and a new treatment and comparing. Randomisation makes the groups alike in everything except the treatment, which removes alternative explanations.

In your job

When a pilot line "improved by 6%", ask three things: compared with what, over how many shifts, and what else changed. A before-and-after comparison without a control line cannot separate the tool from the attention the pilot received.

A p-value of 0.03 means:

  1. There is a 3% chance the new process is better
  2. If nothing had changed, a result this extreme would occur about 3% of the time
  3. The effect is large

It is a statement about the data assuming the null, not about the hypothesis.

Declaring an improvement when there is none is a:

  1. Type I error
  2. Type II error
  3. Sampling bias

A false alarm.

A study of 2 million transactions finds a statistically significant rise in average order value of ₹0.40. The right reaction is:

  1. Roll out immediately
  2. The test must be wrong
  3. Real but probably too small to matter

Significance reflects sample size as much as effect size.

Inequality questions punish one habit above all: forgetting that a variable might be negative.

Rules

  • Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality.
  • Never multiply or divide an inequality by a variable unless you know its sign.
  • |x| is the distance of x from zero. |x − a| is the distance of x from a.
  • |x − a| < b means a − b < x < a + b.
  • |x − a| = b gives two solutions, x = a + b and x = a − b.
  • x² < k² means −k < x < k. x² > k² means x > k or x < −k.

Regression is the most used analytical tool in business. You will rarely run one by hand, but you will often have to decide whether to believe one.

Correlation

The correlation coefficient r runs from −1 to +1. It measures how closely two variables move together in a straight-line pattern. Zero means no linear relationship. It says nothing about how steep the relationship is, and nothing about cause.

Simple regression

Regression fits the line that best predicts one variable from another:

Predicted y = intercept + slope × x

A plant regresses monthly maintenance cost on machine hours and gets:

Maintenance cost (₹ lakh) = 4.0 + 0.015 × machine hours

The intercept, ₹4 lakh, is the cost at zero hours: the fixed component. The slope says each extra machine hour adds ₹1,500. At 1,000 hours the prediction is 4 + 15 = ₹19 lakh. This is how accountants separate fixed from variable costs in practice.

Reading the output

  • R² is the share of variation in y explained by the model. An R² of 0.81 means 81% is explained and r is 0.9.
  • Each coefficient has a standard error and p-value. A slope with a p-value above 0.05 may be zero.
  • Multiple regression uses several predictors. Each coefficient shows the effect of that variable holding the others constant.

Why correlation is not causation

  1. Reverse causation. Plants with more maintenance staff have more breakdowns. The breakdowns caused the hiring.
  2. A third variable. Newer lines have both more automation and fewer defects. Age may explain both.
  3. Selection. The data only includes certain cases.
  4. Chance. Search enough variables and something will correlate.

The remedies are randomised experiments where possible, controlling for obvious third variables, and a plausible mechanism.

Other traps

  • Extrapolation. A line fitted between 600 and 1,400 hours says nothing reliable about 3,000.
  • Outliers. One extreme month can swing the slope.
  • Non-linearity. Always look at the scatter plot before trusting the line.

In your job

Vendor benchmark claims such as "customers using our platform have 20% shorter time to market" are correlations across self-selected companies. Before repeating one, ask what kind of companies adopted it and what else they did. Customers' analysts will ask.

A confident conclusion from a regression. Your job is to find what is wrong with it.

The situation

Deccan Driveline ran an operator training programme at 12 of its 30 production lines last year. The HR head presents this analysis to the managing director:

  • Trained lines averaged a defect rate of 1.8%. Untrained lines averaged 2.9%.
  • A regression of defect rate on training hours per operator across all 30 lines gives a slope of −0.05 percentage points per hour, with a p-value of 0.01 and R² of 0.35.
  • He proposes extending the programme to all lines at ₹2 crore, forecasting a fall of 1.1 points in defect rate everywhere.

The operations head adds two facts. The 12 lines were chosen by plant managers who volunteered. And 9 of the 12 are lines installed in the last four years.

Your questions

  1. What does the regression show, strictly?
  2. Give three alternative explanations for the gap between trained and untrained lines.
  3. What analysis with existing data would be more convincing?
  4. How would you design the rollout so that the effect can be measured?
  5. What would you tell the managing director?
Model answer

1. It shows a statistically significant association: lines with more training hours had lower defect rates, and training hours account for about a third of the variation between lines. It does not show that training caused the difference.

2. Line age: newer lines have better equipment and process control and would have fewer defects anyway. Self-selection: managers who volunteer for training programmes are probably the ones who already run their lines well. Other changes: the trained lines may have had new tooling, different products or more engineering attention in the same year. Any of these could produce the whole gap.

3. Compare each line with itself: the change in defect rate from the year before to the year after, for trained and untrained lines. This "difference in differences" removes fixed differences such as age. Also add line age and product mix to the regression and see whether the training coefficient survives. And compare trained and untrained lines of similar age only: the three older trained lines against older untrained ones.

4. Roll out in phases, choosing the next lines by lottery among those not yet trained. The lines waiting form a natural control group. Measure defect rates for both groups over the same months. This costs nothing extra, because the rollout would take several quarters in any case.

5. "The data are encouraging but cannot yet tell us how much of the gap is due to training. Most trained lines are new and their managers volunteered. I recommend we proceed in phases with lines chosen at random, which lets us measure the real effect within two quarters before committing the full ₹2 crore. The forecast of 1.1 points across all lines is very likely too high, because the older lines differ from those in the pilot."

What the case teaches. Ask how the treated group was chosen before looking at any result. Volunteers and new assets are the two most common sources of flattering pilot results in manufacturing.
