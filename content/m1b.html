ROCE: the ratio managers are judged on

ROCE asks what operating profit the business earns on all the long-term money invested in it, whoever supplied it. If ROCE is higher than the cost of that money (roughly 11 to 13% for a typical Indian manufacturer; you will calculate it properly in Month 3), the company creates value. Kaveri's 19.2% is healthy.

DuPont: taking ROE apart

ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier

The equity multiplier is total assets ÷ equity. For Kaveri: 7.5% × 1.30 × 1.92 = 18.8%. The formula shows three different ways to earn a return:

  • Margin: charge more or spend less. Software and branded goods compete here.
  • Turnover: sell more from the same assets. Retailers and contract manufacturers compete here.
  • Leverage: use more debt. It lifts ROE and raises risk.

Worked example

Suppose Kaveri raises revenue to ₹132 crore on the same assets by using its plant better, at the same net margin. Asset turnover becomes 132 ÷ 92 = 1.43. Profit becomes 7.5% of 132 = ₹9.9 crore, and ROE on the same equity is 9.9 ÷ 48 = 20.6%. Utilisation alone added nearly two points of return.

In your job

Factory simulation and layout work is asset-turnover work: more output from the same floor and machines. Process and quality improvements are margin work. Saying which DuPont lever your proposal moves is how you speak to a general manager.

Whether a purchase is treated as an investment or an expense changes the customer's reported profit, its budget process and who signs. It is the most practical accounting topic for anyone who sells to industry.

Two kinds of spending

  • Capital expenditure (capex) buys something used for more than a year: a robot cell, a building, a perpetual software licence. It goes on the balance sheet as an asset and is charged to profit gradually.
  • Operating expenditure (opex) is used up within the period: salaries, electricity, a software subscription. It is charged to profit immediately.

Depreciation

Depreciation spreads the cost of a physical asset over its useful life. For intangible assets such as software the same idea is called amortisation. The simplest method is straight-line:

Annual depreciation = (Cost − Salvage value) ÷ Useful life

A robot cell costs ₹5 crore, lasts ten years and is expected to be worth ₹0.5 crore at the end. Depreciation is (5 − 0.5) ÷ 10 = ₹0.45 crore a year. After four years its net book value is 5 − 4 × 0.45 = ₹3.2 crore.

Indian tax rules use a different method, written-down value, which charges a fixed percentage of the remaining balance each year. So companies keep one depreciation figure for reporting and another for tax.

  • A plant head measured on EBITDA may prefer capex, because amortisation does not hit EBITDA.
  • A CFO short of cash may prefer the subscription, because year-one cash is ₹0.9 crore and not ₹3 crore.

  • Capex raises capital employed and can lower ROCE in the short term.

In your job

Data sufficiency asks whether you have enough information to answer a question. You do not need the answer itself. Most candidates find the format strange for a week and then find it quick.

The five answers never change

  1. Statement 1 alone is sufficient; statement 2 alone is not.
  2. Statement 2 alone is sufficient; statement 1 alone is not.
  3. Both together are sufficient; neither alone is.
  4. Each statement alone is sufficient.
  5. Even together they are not sufficient.

Method

  • Test statement 1 by itself. Then forget it completely and test statement 2 by itself. Combine them only if neither works alone.
  • For a "what is the value" question, sufficient means exactly one value.
  • For a yes/no question, sufficient means always yes or always no. "Always no" is sufficient.
  • Try to break each statement with unusual numbers: zero, negatives, fractions, equal values.
  • Stop as soon as you know the answer can be found. Do not finish the calculation.

Practice

The options below are the five standard answers.

What is the value of x? (1) 2x + y = 10. (2) y = 4.

  1. A: statement 1 alone
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. C: both together
  4. D: each alone
  5. E: not sufficient

One equation with two unknowns is not enough. Statement 2 says nothing about x. Together, x = 3.

Is the integer n even? (1) 3n is even. (2) n + 1 is odd.

  1. A: statement 1 alone
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. C: both together
  4. D: each alone
  5. E: not sufficient

3 is odd, so 3n is even only if n is even. If n + 1 is odd then n is even. Each works alone.

What was a plant's profit in March? (1) March revenue was ₹50 lakh higher than February revenue. (2) March costs were 80% of March revenue.

  1. A: statement 1 alone
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. C: both together
  4. D: each alone
  5. E: not sufficient

Together you know profit is 20% of March revenue, but March revenue is still unknown because February's is.

Is x > y? (1) x − y = 3. (2) x² > y².

  1. A: statement 1 alone
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. C: both together
  4. D: each alone
  5. E: not sufficient

Statement 1 means x is 3 more than y, always yes. Statement 2 fails: x = −5, y = 2 satisfies it with x smaller.

How long do pipes A and B take to fill a tank together? (1) A alone fills it in 6 hours. (2) B fills twice as fast as A.

  1. A: statement 1 alone
  2. B: statement 2 alone
  3. C: both together
  4. D: each alone
  5. E: not sufficient

You need both rates. Together: A takes 6 hours, B takes 3, so combined they take 2 hours.

An annual report can run to 300 pages. You need about eight of them. This session gives you a reading order that works for any listed company.

What is inside

  • Chairman's or CEO's letter: priorities in the company's own words. Good for language to reuse in a proposal.
  • Management discussion and analysis (MD&A): management's explanation of the year, the industry and risks.
  • Financial statements: the three statements, in two versions. Standalone covers the parent company only. Consolidated includes all subsidiaries. Use consolidated unless you are studying one legal entity.
  • Notes to the accounts: the detail behind each line. Segment reporting, borrowings, capital commitments and related-party transactions live here.
  • Auditor's report: an independent opinion. "Unmodified" is clean. A "qualified" opinion or an "emphasis of matter" paragraph deserves a careful read.

A twenty-minute routine

  1. Five-year summary (2 minutes). Most reports have one. Is revenue growing? Are margins stable?
  2. Income statement (3 minutes). Revenue growth, operating margin, any large "other income" or "exceptional items" that flatter profit.
  3. Cash flow statement (4 minutes). Is cash from operations close to profit after tax over several years? How large is capex? Is the company borrowing or repaying?
  4. Balance sheet (3 minutes). Debt against equity, and whether receivables and inventory are growing faster than sales.
  5. Segment note (3 minutes). Which business or region earns the money.
  6. MD&A and letter (5 minutes). What management says it will invest in next: capacity, new plants, digital programmes, sustainability targets.

Warning signs

  • Profit rises year after year while cash from operations does not.
  • Receivables grow much faster than revenue.
  • A large share of profit comes from "other income" such as asset sales.
  • Frequent changes of auditor or accounting policy.
  • Large transactions with companies owned by the promoters.

Worked example: capital work-in-progress

Indian balance sheets show a line called capital work-in-progress (CWIP): money already spent on plants and lines that are not yet operating. A jump in CWIP from ₹200 crore to ₹900 crore tells you a large project is under construction. For someone who sells factory simulation or commissioning services, that single line says the customer is about to launch new capacity.

In your job

Before any account workshop, read the customer's MD&A and capex plans. Opening with "your report says you are adding a second line at Sanand by next year" earns more attention than any product slide.

Reading comprehension passages are dense and often outside your field. The skill being tested is following an argument's structure, which you can do without subject knowledge.

Method

  • Read for structure. After each paragraph, say in five words what it did: "gives history", "states general pattern", "draws implication".
  • Find the main point. It is usually where the author moves from description to a claim.
  • For detail questions, go back and find the line. The right answer restates the passage in other words.
  • For inference questions, choose what must follow from the text. Reject anything that needs outside knowledge or goes further than the author did.
  • Be suspicious of extreme wording such as "always", "never", "proves".

Passage

When factories first adopted electric motors in the 1890s, managers expected immediate gains. For roughly three decades the gains were small. Early adopters simply replaced the central steam engine with one large electric motor and kept the existing arrangement of shafts and belts, so machines stayed where the power transmission system required them to be. Productivity rose sharply only in the 1920s, when a new generation of plants gave each machine its own small motor. That change allowed machines to be arranged according to the flow of work, buildings to be single-storey and better lit, and sections of a plant to be stopped without halting the rest.

Economists use this history to explain a pattern seen with later general-purpose technologies. The technology itself is seldom the binding constraint. The returns arrive when organisations redesign processes, layouts, skills and decision rights around what the technology makes possible, and such redesign is slow, costly and hard to measure. During the redesign period, spending on these changes is recorded as cost while the benefits lie in the future, so measured productivity can stagnate or even fall before it rises.

The argument has a practical implication for managers assessing a new technology. A disappointing early result is weak evidence that the technology has failed, although it is not evidence of success either. The more useful question is whether the complementary changes are under way.

The main point of the passage is that:

  1. Electric motors were a poor investment for early factories
  2. The gains from a general-purpose technology tend to arrive late, because they depend on slow organisational redesign
  3. Productivity statistics are unreliable and should be ignored
  4. Managers in the 1890s were less capable than those in the 1920s

Paragraph 1 gives the example, paragraph 2 the general pattern, paragraph 3 the implication. Only (b) covers all three.

According to the passage, early electrified factories saw small gains because they:

  1. Used motors that were too small
  2. Could not afford electricity
  3. Kept the layout dictated by the old power transmission system
  4. Built single-storey buildings

The passage says they kept the shafts and belts, so machines stayed where they were.

It can be inferred that a firm which adds an advanced planning tool but leaves its planning process and responsibilities unchanged would most likely:

  1. See limited benefit until it redesigns how planning is done
  2. See immediate large gains
  3. Find that the tool has failed permanently
  4. Reduce its costs in the first year

This matches the early-adopter pattern. Option (c) contradicts the last paragraph, which says early results are weak evidence of failure.

The author adds "although it is not evidence of success either" in order to:

  1. Argue that new technologies usually fail
  2. Criticise economists who study productivity
  3. Suggest that early results should be the main test
  4. Avoid implying that every slow start will end well

The phrase limits the claim. The author is saying early results are uninformative in both directions.

Financial accounting reports to outsiders. Managerial accounting helps insiders decide. Its first question is how costs change when volume changes.

Fixed and variable costs

  • Variable costs rise in step with output: material, bought-out parts, piece-rate labour, energy per unit.
  • Fixed costs stay the same over a range of output: rent, salaried staff, depreciation, software subscriptions.

Over a long enough time every cost can change, and many costs are mixed. The split is still the most useful simplification in management.

Contribution margin

Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit

Each unit sold contributes this amount, first towards covering fixed costs and then to profit.

Kaveri's standard fixture line sells fixtures at ₹8 lakh each. Variable cost is ₹5 lakh. Contribution is ₹3 lakh per fixture, or 37.5% of price. Fixed costs for the line are ₹600 lakh a year (₹6 crore).

Break-even

Break-even volume = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit

600 ÷ 3 = 200 fixtures. Below 200 the line loses money. At 200 it earns nothing. Break-even revenue is 200 × 8 = ₹1,600 lakh, or ₹16 crore.

Margin of safety and operating leverage

At 260 fixtures, sales can fall by 60 units, or 23%, before the line makes a loss. That is the margin of safety.

Now compare 260 with 286 fixtures, a 10% rise in volume. Profit rises from 180 to 258, which is 43%. The multiplier is called the degree of operating leverage and equals contribution ÷ profit: 780 ÷ 180 = 4.3. High fixed costs make profit very sensitive to volume in both directions. This explains the Week 1 case, and it explains why capital-intensive plants chase utilisation.

Decisions this supports

  • Special orders. With spare capacity, an order priced above variable cost adds profit even if it is below full cost.
  • Automation. Automating usually swaps variable cost for fixed cost. It raises contribution per unit and raises the break-even point, so it pays only above a certain volume.
  • Make or buy. Compare the supplier's price with the costs you would actually avoid.

Direct costs are easy to trace to a product. Overheads are not, and the way a company spreads them decides which products look profitable. Engineering change is one of the largest hidden overheads.

The parts of product cost

  • Direct material and direct labour: traced to each product.
  • Manufacturing overhead: everything else in the factory, such as supervision, maintenance, engineering support, material handling, quality and depreciation.

Traditional allocation

Most companies spread overhead using one driver, commonly direct labour hours or machine hours. Kaveri has ₹240 lakh of overhead and two product families. Standard fixtures use 30,000 labour hours and custom robotic cells use 10,000.

The overhead rate is 240 ÷ 40,000 = ₹600 per labour hour. Standard fixtures are charged ₹180 lakh and custom cells ₹60 lakh.

  • Low-volume, high-variety products are usually under-costed by simple allocation.

Rate questions reward one habit: write everything as an amount per unit of time, then add the rates.

Core rules

  • Work: if a machine finishes a job in 6 hours, its rate is 1/6 of the job per hour. Rates of machines working together add. Times do not.
  • Distance = speed × time. Average speed is total distance ÷ total time, never the average of the speeds.
  • Average = sum ÷ count, so sum = average × count. Most average questions are solved by working with sums.
  • Weighted average: multiply each value by its weight, add, divide by total weight. The result sits closer to the value with more weight.
  • Proportional work: output = workers × hours × rate per worker-hour.

Worked example

Machine A does a job in 6 hours, machine B in 3 hours. Rates are 1/6 and 1/3. Together: 1/6 + 2/6 = 1/2 of the job per hour, so 2 hours.

A budget is a plan expressed in money. A variance is the gap between plan and reality. A business case is a small budget for one decision. Senior managers spend much of their time on these three things.

Budgets

Each year a company sets targets for sales, costs, capex and cash. Budgets do three jobs: they allocate resources, they coordinate departments, and they are the yardstick managers are measured against. A proposal that arrives after the budget is fixed has to displace something already approved.

Variance analysis

When actual cost differs from budget, split the gap by cause. Kaveri budgeted material for 200 fixtures at ₹2.0 lakh each: ₹400 lakh. It actually built 220 fixtures at ₹2.1 lakh each: ₹462 lakh. The total variance is ₹62 lakh over budget.

CauseCalculation₹ lakh
Volume: built 20 more fixtures20 × 2.040
Price: each cost ₹0.1 lakh more220 × 0.122
Total variance62

The ₹40 lakh is not a problem, since more fixtures were sold. The ₹22 lakh is the real overrun and belongs to purchasing or to scrap. Splitting a variance turns "we overspent" into a question someone can answer.

The skeleton of a business case

  1. The problem and its cost today. A baseline in money.
  2. The investment. One-time costs and recurring costs, including the customer's own effort.
  3. The benefits. Each one tied to a P&L or balance-sheet line, with the assumption stated.
  4. The measures. Payback and return on investment now; net present value from Month 3.
  5. Sensitivity. What happens if benefits are half as large or arrive late.
  6. Risks and what must be true.

Two simple measures

Payback period = Initial investment ÷ Net annual benefit

ROI over n years = (Total net benefits − Investment) ÷ Investment

An investment of ₹1.5 crore that returns a net ₹1.3 crore a year pays back in 1.5 ÷ 1.3 = 1.15 years, about 14 months. Over three years ROI is (3.9 − 1.5) ÷ 1.5 = 160%.

Both measures ignore the fact that money received later is worth less than money today. They are still what most plant-level approvals use, and many Indian manufacturers expect payback within two to three years for operational projects.

Hard and soft benefits

Finance teams sort benefits into those that reduce cash spending or bring in cash (hard) and those that free time or reduce risk (soft). A case that rests only on soft benefits rarely survives. State hard benefits conservatively and list soft ones separately.

This is the month's capstone case. Build the business case yourself before reading the model answer.

The situation

Sahyadri Auto Components is a fictional tier-1 supplier of welded seat and chassis structures with plants in Pune and Chennai. It launches two new robotic welding lines a year for new vehicle programmes. Its manufacturing engineering head, Farhan Shaikh, wants to adopt virtual commissioning: testing robot programs and PLC logic against a simulated line before the real one is installed.

The CFO, Lakshmi Iyer, has asked for a one-page business case. She has told Farhan she will not accept "improved quality" or "better collaboration" as benefits.

Farhan gathers these facts:

  • On-site commissioning of a line currently takes 10 weeks. Vendors and two pilot users suggest virtual commissioning could cut this by 4 weeks.
  • Each week a line starts production late costs Sahyadri about ₹15 lakh in lost contribution.
  • Rework of fixtures and robot programs during commissioning costs about ₹60 lakh a year. Farhan expects this to halve.
  • The investment is ₹1.5 crore in the first year for software, hardware, integration services and training.
  • Running cost is ₹20 lakh a year for subscriptions and one simulation engineer's time.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the gross annual benefit, the net annual benefit, the payback period and the three-year ROI.
  2. Lakshmi asks, "What if you only get half the benefits?" Recalculate payback and three-year ROI.
  3. How many weeks per launch must be saved for the project to pay back within three years, keeping the rework saving as assumed?
  4. Which benefit is hard and which will Lakshmi challenge? What evidence would you bring?
  5. Write the two-sentence recommendation Farhan should open with.
Model answer

1. Time saving: 2 launches × 4 weeks × ₹15 lakh = ₹120 lakh. Rework saving: half of 60 = ₹30 lakh. Gross benefit ₹150 lakh a year. Less running cost of 20: net benefit ₹130 lakh, or ₹1.3 crore. Payback = 150 ÷ 130 = 1.15 years, about 14 months. Three-year ROI = (3 × 130 − 150) ÷ 150 = 160%.

2. Half the benefits is ₹75 lakh gross, ₹55 lakh net. Payback = 150 ÷ 55 = 2.7 years. Three-year ROI = (165 − 150) ÷ 150 = 10%. The project still recovers its cost within three years, but only just.

3. A three-year payback needs a net benefit of 150 ÷ 3 = ₹50 lakh a year, so a gross benefit of ₹70 lakh. Rework provides 30, leaving 40 from time savings. 40 ÷ (2 launches × 15) = 1.3 weeks per launch. The case holds if a third of the promised time saving is achieved.

4. The rework saving is hard: it is money currently spent on fixture modification and programming hours, visible in project accounts. The lost-contribution figure will be challenged. Lakshmi will ask whether a late line really loses sales or whether the customer simply takes delivery later, and whether ₹15 lakh a week is contribution or revenue. Farhan should bring records of the last four launches showing actual delay, penalties or premium freight paid, and overtime used to catch up. Contractual late-delivery penalties are the strongest evidence because they are cash.

5. "I am asking for ₹1.5 crore to commission our welding lines virtually, which on current evidence returns ₹1.3 crore a year and pays back in about 14 months. Even if we achieve only a third of the expected time saving, it pays back within three years, and I propose we prove it on the next Chennai launch before buying for both plants."

What the case teaches. A credible case leads with the answer, separates hard from soft benefits, shows the downside before being asked, and finds the break-even assumption. The break-even of 1.3 weeks is more persuasive than the headline 160%, because it tells the decision-maker how wrong the estimate can be.

Data Insights questions bury a simple calculation inside a table, chart or set of documents. Speed comes from reading the question first and touching only the data it needs.

Method

  • Read titles, units and column headings before any numbers. Check whether figures are in units, thousands or percentages.
  • Read the question, then find the two or three cells you need.
  • Estimate before you calculate. Options are often far enough apart that rounding is safe.
  • Watch for the difference between a count and a rate. The plant with the most defects may not have the highest defect rate.
  • In the real test, tables can be sorted by column. Sort by the column the question is about.
