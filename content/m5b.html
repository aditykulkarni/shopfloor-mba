You will have met lean tools on shop floors. An MBA course treats lean as a management system with an economic logic, and asks why so many companies that copied the tools failed to get the results.

The aim

Shorten the time between a customer order and payment by removing everything that adds no value. From Little's law, shorter time means less inventory. From the queueing formula, short time at high utilisation is possible only with low variability. Lean is a long campaign against variability and delay.

Two pillars

  • Just-in-time: make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. Its tools are takt time, continuous flow, pull systems and levelled production.
  • Jidoka: build quality in. Stop when a problem occurs, so defects are not passed on and causes are found while fresh. Its tools are the andon cord and mistake-proofing.

Seven wastes

Overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion and defects. Toyota's Taiichi Ohno regarded overproduction as the worst, because it creates most of the others.

Key practices

PracticeWhat it doesEconomic effect

Quick changeover (SMED)Cuts set-up time from hours to minutesSmaller batches, less inventory, more flexibility

Why imitation often fails

  • Tools without the system. Kanban in an unstable process produces shortages.
  • Inventory cut first. Stock removed before variability is reduced exposes the plant, as last week's passage argued.
  • No stop-and-fix culture. If stopping the line is punished, problems stay hidden.
  • Measures that contradict it. Utilisation targets and absorption costing reward overproduction.
  • Treating it as a cost-cutting project. People will not suggest improvements that remove their own jobs.

The last two are management failures. Lean succeeds or fails on how managers measure and behave.

Lean and digital

Digital tools can strengthen lean: simulation tests a levelled schedule before it is tried, and digital work instructions support standard work. They can also automate waste. The test is whether lead time and variability fall.

In your job

Lean practitioners in a customer's plant can be your strongest allies or your sharpest critics. Speak their language: show how a layout or process plan changes lead time, work-in-progress and travel distance on a value stream map, before mentioning software.

Quality has a cost structure, and most of the cost is invisible in ordinary accounts. Making it visible is how quality gets investment.

A common rule of thumb is that a defect costs about ten times more at each later stage: 1 to prevent, 10 to catch in the plant, 100 after it reaches the customer. The exact ratios vary, and the direction does not. Money moved from failure to prevention usually pays back several times. In many manufacturers the total cost of quality runs to several percent of sales, most of it failure cost buried in overhead.

Common cause and special cause

Every process varies. Common-cause variation is the normal noise of a stable process. Special-cause variation comes from something specific: a worn tool, a new material batch. A control chart plots a measure over time with limits at three standard deviations from the mean. Points inside the limits are noise. A point outside, or a clear pattern, signals a special cause worth investigating.

The discipline this teaches managers is restraint. Reacting to every fluctuation, which Deming called tampering, makes a stable process worse. Not every bad month has a cause that someone should be blamed for.

Process capability

Capability compares what the customer allows with what the process does.

Cp = (upper spec limit − lower spec limit) ÷ (6 × standard deviation)

Cpk = distance from the mean to the nearer spec limit ÷ (3 × standard deviation)

A shaft is specified at 10.00 ± 0.06 mm. The process has a standard deviation of 0.015 mm.

  • Cp = 0.12 ÷ 0.09 = 1.33. The process is narrow enough.
  • If the mean has drifted to 10.02, the nearer limit is 0.04 away. Cpk = 0.04 ÷ 0.045 = 0.89. The process is off-centre and makes defects.

Cp tells you whether the process could meet the specification. Cpk tells you whether it does. Automotive customers commonly require a Cpk of at least 1.33.

Yield through a chain

Five process steps each with a 98% first-pass yield give 0.98⁵ = 90.4% overall. This is rolled throughput yield, and it is the reliability arithmetic of Month 2 applied to quality.

Quality at the source

Inspection finds defects after the cost is incurred. Prevention means the design, the process and the operator's method make the defect hard to produce. Product and process design decide most of a product's eventual quality cost, which is why design for manufacturing and early process validation have high returns.

Lean attacks delay. Six Sigma attacks variation. Both rest on a disciplined way of solving problems that is useful far beyond the factory.

What Six Sigma means

The name refers to a process so capable that the nearest specification limit is six standard deviations from the mean. Allowing for drift over time, that corresponds to about 3.4 defects per million opportunities. In practice Six Sigma is a project method run by trained specialists, called belts, and chosen for financial impact.

DMAIC

  1. Define the problem, the customer and the financial stake.
  2. Measure current performance and check that the measurement system can be trusted.
  3. Analyse to find root causes, using data.
  4. Improve by testing and implementing solutions.
  5. Control so the gain is held: standard work, control charts, ownership.

The order protects against the most common failure in organisations, which is jumping to a solution before the problem is understood. The control step protects against the second, which is improvement that fades.

Root cause tools

  • Five whys. Ask why repeatedly until you reach a cause you can act on. The machine stopped. Why? A fuse blew. Why? A bearing was overloaded. Why? It was not lubricated. Why? The lubrication pump failed. Why? Its shaft was worn because swarf got in. Fit a strainer.
  • Fishbone diagram. Organise possible causes under headings such as method, machine, material, people, measurement and environment.
  • Pareto analysis. Rank causes by frequency or cost. A few usually account for most of the problem.
  • Designed experiments. Change several factors in a planned pattern to find which matter and how they interact.

The A3

Toyota's version is a single sheet covering background, current condition, goal, root-cause analysis, countermeasures, plan and follow-up. Fitting the thinking onto one page forces clarity, and the sheet doubles as a tool for coaching and for getting agreement.

Where these methods mislead

  • They suit stable, repeated processes. For new products or markets there is little data to analyse.
  • They can become certification programmes that count projects and belts instead of results.
  • They improve the existing process and seldom ask whether the process should exist.

In your job

DMAIC is a ready-made structure for a consulting engagement or a discovery workshop. Define and Measure are what a good requirements phase does. If a customer cannot state current performance, the first deliverable is a baseline.

Two more critical reasoning types. "Explain" questions present two facts that seem to conflict and ask what reconciles them. "Flaw" questions ask you to name the error in an argument.

Method

  • Explain: state the surprise in your own words, then look for a fact that makes both sides true. Do not pick an answer that disputes one of the facts.
  • Flaw: find the gap, then choose the option that describes it in general terms. Common flaws are confusing correlation with cause, generalising from a small or biased sample, and treating a necessary condition as a sufficient one.

A supply chain is every organisation and step between raw material and the final customer. Its design is a strategic choice, and the commonest error is to run one kind of chain for the wrong kind of product.

Two kinds of product

Marshall Fisher's framework sorts products by how predictable their demand is.

Functional productsInnovative products
DemandStable, predictableUncertain, short life
MarginLowHigh
VarietyLowHigh
Main cost of a mismatchExcess costStock-outs and markdowns
Right supply chainEfficient: low cost, high utilisation, minimal inventoryResponsive: spare capacity, buffers, speed

Standard fasteners need an efficient chain. A new electric scooter model with uncertain demand needs a responsive one. A firm that applies cost-minimising rules to an innovative product will run short during the launch and be left with obsolete stock afterwards.

Push, pull and the decoupling point

Upstream of some point, activity is driven by forecast (push). Downstream of it, by actual orders (pull). That point is where inventory is held. Engineer-to-order machine builders pull almost everything. Consumer goods are pushed to the shelf. Moving the point upstream reduces forecast risk and lengthens the customer's wait. Modular design lets a company hold generic modules and configure to order.

The bullwhip effect

Small changes in end-customer demand grow into large swings further up the chain. A 5% rise at the retailer can become a 40% rise in orders at the component maker. The causes identified by Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang:

  • Forecast updating. Each tier adds its own safety margin to the tier below's orders.
  • Order batching. Monthly ordering turns smooth demand into spikes.
  • Price fluctuations. Promotions make buyers stock up and then stop.
  • Shortage gaming. When supply is rationed, customers over-order and later cancel.

The remedies attack each cause: share real end-demand data, order more often in smaller lots, keep prices stable, and allocate scarce supply by past sales and not by order size. Shorter lead times help everything.

Why this matters upstream

Capital-goods makers sit at the far end of the whip. A modest slowdown in car sales becomes a collapse in orders for machine tools and fixtures. This is the operating reason behind the high betas you met in Month 3.

In your job

Your customers' investment budgets are a bullwhip signal. Watching end-market data, such as vehicle registrations or consumer-goods volumes, gives earlier warning of a freeze or a thaw in capital spending than waiting for the customer to say so.

Three topics that take up a growing share of senior operations leaders' time.

Supply chain risk

Risks fall into two kinds. Recurrent risks are the everyday mismatches of supply and demand, handled with forecasting and buffers. Disruption risks are rare and severe: a fire, a flood, a port closure, a supplier's bankruptcy, a trade restriction.

For disruptions, think in expected value and in survival, as in Month 2. A risk with a 3% yearly chance and a ₹100 crore impact has an expected cost of ₹3 crore a year. If ₹100 crore would threaten the company, the expected value understates how much protection is worth.

Ways to build resilience

  • Redundancy: extra stock, spare capacity, second sources. Certain cost, uncertain payoff.
  • Flexibility: standard parts, interchangeable plants, cross-trained people, designs that accept alternative components. Usually cheaper, and useful every day.
  • Visibility: knowing who your suppliers' suppliers are and where they are located.
  • Speed of response: clear authority and rehearsed plans.

The practical test for each critical part: how long could we survive without it, and how long would it take to recover supply? Where recovery takes longer than survival, there is a gap to close.

Sales and operations planning

S&OP is a monthly process in which sales, operations, finance and product management agree one plan for the next 12 to 24 months. Without it, sales forecasts one number, the plant builds to another and finance budgets a third. The steps are a demand review, a supply review, reconciliation of gaps, and an executive decision meeting. Its value lies in forcing trade-offs to be made deliberately and early. It is also where a rising manager first sees how the functions of a company collide.

Sustainability in operations

  • Emissions accounting uses three scopes: a company's direct emissions, those from purchased energy, and those across its supply chain and product use. For manufacturers the third is usually the largest and the hardest to measure.
  • Customer pressure. Vehicle makers increasingly ask suppliers for product carbon data and reduction plans, so it is becoming a condition of doing business.
  • Regulation. Rules such as the European Union's carbon border mechanism affect exporters of steel, aluminium and similar goods. The details change, so check current requirements.
  • Economics. Energy and material efficiency usually pay for themselves. Deeper decarbonisation often does not without a carbon price or a customer premium, and the honest business case says so.

In your job

Product carbon footprints depend on bills of material, routings and supplier data, which already live in PLM and manufacturing systems. This is a growing reason for customers to invest, and the value argument rests on market access and compliance more than on cost saving.

A resilience decision, with numbers and a judgement beyond them.

The situation

Deccan Driveline buys a critical forging alloy from one overseas mill. Purchasing wants to keep the single source because volume discounts save ₹2 crore a year compared with splitting the business. The operations head wants a second, domestic source after a near miss last year.

The risk team estimates a 5% chance each year of a disruption at the mill lasting several weeks. With a single source the loss would be about ₹60 crore in missed sales and penalties. With a qualified second source already supplying 30%, the loss would be about ₹15 crore.

A third idea is to stay single-sourced and hold two weeks of extra stock, costing ₹1.5 crore a year to carry, which would reduce the loss to about ₹35 crore.

Deccan's EBIT is ₹200 crore.

Your questions

  1. Calculate the expected annual cost of each option, including what each costs to maintain.
  2. At what disruption probability are single and dual sourcing equal?
  3. How large is the worst case under each option relative to EBIT?
  4. What is left out of these figures?
  5. What do you recommend?
Model answer

1. Single source: 0.05 × 60 = ₹3.0 crore expected loss, no extra cost. Dual source: ₹2 crore of lost discount plus 0.05 × 15 = 0.75, total ₹2.75 crore. Single with stock: 1.5 + 0.05 × 35 = ₹3.25 crore. On expected cost the options are close, with dual sourcing slightly ahead.

2. Dual sourcing costs ₹2 crore for certain and saves 45 when a disruption occurs. They are equal when p × 45 = 2, so p = 4.4%. The estimate of 5% is barely above that, and such estimates are rough.

3. Single: ₹60 crore, which is 30% of a year's EBIT. Dual: ₹15 crore, 7.5%. Stock: ₹35 crore, 17.5%.

4. Customer consequences beyond penalties: a tier-1 that stops a vehicle maker's line may lose the next programme. Benefits of a second source in normal times: a price benchmark, faster response to design changes from a domestic mill, and currency diversification. Costs of dual sourcing beyond the discount: qualification effort, and possible quality differences between mills. And the probability may not be constant. Geopolitical or climate risk could be rising.

5. Choose dual sourcing. The expected costs are about equal, so the decision turns on the tail: avoiding a loss of 30% of annual profit, and the customer damage on top, is worth far more than the small difference in averages. Give the second source 20 to 30% of volume so that it stays qualified and interested. Negotiate with the primary mill to keep most of the discount in exchange for a longer contract.

What the case teaches. When the expected values are close, look at the worst case and at what the calculation left out. Resilience spending looks like waste in every year when nothing happens, which is why it needs a decision at senior level and cannot be left to a purchasing measure.
