The word strategy is attached to almost everything in business. Used precisely, it means a small number of connected choices about where to compete and how to win, including what not to do.

Strategy is different from being efficient

Michael Porter separates two things. Operational effectiveness means doing the same activities better than rivals: lean, quality, automation. It is necessary, and competitors copy it, so its gains pass to customers. Strategy means doing different activities, or doing them differently, to deliver a distinct kind of value.

The test of a strategy is whether it involves trade-offs. A firm that chooses to serve tier-1 suppliers with guaranteed commissioning dates has to hold engineering capacity that a low-price fixture shop does not. It cannot also be the cheapest. If a stated strategy rules nothing out, it is a list of ambitions.

Rumelt's kernel

Richard Rumelt gives the most practical definition. A good strategy has three parts.

  1. Diagnosis: what is going on. A clear statement of the main challenge.
  2. Guiding policy: the overall approach chosen to deal with it.
  3. Coherent actions: steps that reinforce one another and carry out the policy.

Bad strategy, in his terms, is fluff, failure to face the problem, goals mistaken for strategy ("grow 20% a year"), and long lists of unconnected initiatives.

Three levels

  • Corporate strategy: which businesses to be in.
  • Business strategy: how to compete within one industry. This is most of what follows.
  • Functional strategy: how operations, marketing, finance and the rest support the business strategy.

The questions of a business strategy

A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin reduce it to five linked choices: What is our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win there? What capabilities must we have? What management systems do we need? The second and third are the heart of it, and they are the marketing choices of Month 4 seen from the top.

An example

Diagnosis: Kaveri's standard fixtures are being undercut by low-cost shops, and its custom cells lose money on engineering changes. Guiding policy: become the supplier that guarantees start-of-production dates for tier-1 structural suppliers. Coherent actions: validate every cell virtually, price commissioning as part of the contract, standardise modules to cut change cost, decline one-off bargain fixture orders, and build a service team near the two main customer clusters.

Each action makes the others more effective, and a competitor would have to copy all of them.

In your job

Listen for the kernel in a customer's own strategy. Their annual report and leadership messages state a diagnosis and a guiding policy, sometimes clearly. A proposal that names both and shows itself as one of the coherent actions gets attention from senior people that a list of features never will.

According to Porter, lasting advantage comes mainly from:

  1. Being more efficient at the same activities as rivals
  2. Performing different activities, or the same ones differently, with trade-offs
  3. Growing faster than the market

Efficiency gains are copied. Distinct positions with trade-offs are harder to imitate.

"Become the market leader with 25% growth" is, in Rumelt's terms:

  1. A diagnosis
  2. A guiding policy
  3. A goal mistaken for a strategy

It says what is wanted and nothing about the challenge or the approach.

A sign that a strategy involves real choice is that it:

  1. Makes clear what the firm will not do
  2. Appeals to every customer
  3. Lists many initiatives

Trade-offs are what make a position hard to copy.

Applying it

  1. Define the industry carefully: which products, which region. "Manufacturing" is too broad. "Welding fixtures for Indian automotive suppliers" works.
  2. List the players under each force.
  3. Judge each force as strong, moderate or weak, with reasons.
  4. Decide which one or two forces set the industry's profitability.
  5. Ask how each is changing and how a firm could position itself where the forces are weakest.

Misuses

  • Treating it as a checklist to fill in, without reaching a conclusion.
  • Describing the industry today and ignoring how the forces are shifting.
  • Assuming growth means profit. Fast-growing industries attract entrants and can be unprofitable for years.
  • Forgetting complements: products that raise the value of yours, as fixtures and robots do for each other.

The value chain

Porter's value chain breaks a company into the activities that create value.

  • Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service.
  • Support activities: procurement, technology development, human resources, firm infrastructure.

Advantage comes from performing particular activities at lower cost or in a way customers will pay more for, and from how the activities fit together. Mapping a firm's value chain against a rival's shows where the two actually differ.

Fit

The strongest positions rest on many activities that reinforce one another. A competitor can copy one activity. Copying a whole system is much harder. If each of five linked activities can be imitated with a 70% chance, the chance of matching all five is 0.7⁵, about 17%.

Profit pools

An industry's total profit is spread unevenly along its chain of stages. The stage with the most revenue is often not the one with the most profit.

Stage of an automation project (illustrative)Share of customer spendTypical margin
Robots and standard hardware40%Low to moderate
Fixtures and tooling20%Low
Integration and commissioning25%Moderate, volatile
Engineering software5%High
Service, spares and upgrades over life10% a year of installed valueHigh

The figures are for illustration, and the pattern is common. Profit concentrates where there is differentiation, recurring revenue and switching cost: software and after-sales service. Hardware makers have spent twenty years trying to move into those pools.

Where profit migrates

Pools shift over time. When a product becomes a commodity, profit moves to adjacent stages: to components that remain scarce, to integration, to data and services. Asking "where will profit be in five years?" is more useful than asking where revenue is today.

Vertical scope

The value chain also frames the choice of which stages to own. Owning a stage makes sense when it is a profit pool you can win, or when depending on outsiders for it is dangerous. Month 5's make-or-buy logic is the same question at the level of a single part.

The situation

  • About 40 firms in western India build welding fixtures. Six can build complete robotic cells.
  • A capable toolroom with CAD seats and a few machining centres can start making simple fixtures for under ₹5 crore.
  • Kaveri's top five customers give it 70% of revenue. They run tenders and expect yearly price reductions.
  • Robots come from four global makers. Steel, pneumatics and controls come from many sources.
  • Some large tier-1s have their own toolrooms and build fixtures in-house when they have spare capacity.
  • Customers increasingly ask for a line that is commissioned and running by a fixed date, with penalties for delay.

Your questions

  1. Rate each of the five forces for simple fixtures and give your reasons.
  2. Repeat for complete robotic cells delivered against a start-of-production date.
  3. Which force matters most in each segment?
  4. What does this imply about where Kaveri should compete?

Model answer

1. Simple fixtures. Rivalry: strong, with 40 similar firms. Entry: strong threat, since ₹5 crore buys a start. Buyers: very strong, concentrated and tendering. Suppliers: weak, as inputs are widely available. Substitutes: strong, because customers can build in-house. Four of five forces are against the industry. Profits will stay thin whatever a firm does.

2. Complete cells with a date commitment. Rivalry: moderate, with six capable firms. Entry: weak to moderate, because it takes controls, robotics and commissioning skill, references, and a balance sheet that can carry penalties. Buyers: still strong, but they cannot easily switch mid-programme and they value certainty above a small price difference. Suppliers: moderate, since robot makers have some power and sometimes integrate themselves. Substitutes: weak, since few customers can build a full cell in-house.

3. For simple fixtures, buyer power combined with easy entry. For cells, buyer power softened by the cost to the buyer of a late launch.

4. The two segments are almost different industries. Kaveri earns its return in cells and gives it back in simple fixtures, which matches the cross-subsidy that activity-based costing revealed in Month 1, where the under-costed product was the custom cell. Before shifting, Kaveri must first price cells to their true cost. Then it should build its position where the forces are weaker: date-guaranteed cells.

5. Reduce customer concentration by adding tier-1s in a second industry, such as two-wheelers or appliances, so that no account can dictate terms. Raise switching costs and differentiation: standard modules reused across a customer's lines, service contracts, and virtual validation records that make Kaveri the low-risk choice for the next programme. Either move makes the buyer's threat to walk away less credible.

What the case teaches. Define the industry narrowly enough and the analysis becomes useful. A company often straddles segments with very different economics, and seeing that is the first step of strategy.

A firm has a competitive advantage when it sustains higher profit than its rivals. That can only come from one of two places.

The value wedge

Picture a vertical line. At the top is the customer's willingness to pay. At the bottom is the cost of supplying the product, including what suppliers and employees must be paid. Price sits somewhere between.

  • Willingness to pay minus price is the value the customer keeps.
  • Price minus cost is the value the firm keeps.

The total, willingness to pay minus cost, is the value created. A firm has an advantage when its wedge is wider than competitors'. There are two ways to widen it.

Two generic positions

  • Differentiation: raise willingness to pay by more than it costs to do so. Reliability, performance, service, brand, integration.
  • Cost leadership: lower cost by more than any loss in willingness to pay. Scale, process, design for manufacture, location.

Either can also be applied to a narrow segment, which Porter calls focus.

Stuck in the middle

A firm that is neither the cheapest nor clearly better is vulnerable from both sides. The activities needed for the lowest cost and those needed for the highest quality often conflict. A few firms have managed to be better and cheaper at once through a new process or business model, and those cases are rare and usually temporary.

Two wider questions

Any advantage worth having attracts imitators. The strategic question is what stops them.

The resource-based view

Jay Barney's test for whether a resource or capability can support lasting advantage is often remembered as VRIO.

  • Valuable: does it raise willingness to pay or lower cost?
  • Rare: do few competitors have it?
  • Inimitable: is it costly to copy or substitute?
  • Organised: is the firm set up to exploit it?

Machines and software licences fail the test, because anyone can buy them. What passes is usually built over time: accumulated know-how, reputation, relationships, culture, data.

Switching costs, more closely

This is the power you work inside. Enterprise engineering software holds customers through data in proprietary formats, integrations, customisations, trained users and validated processes. The effect is high retention and resilient pricing. The limits: switching costs protect existing customers and do not win new ones, and they turn into resentment if a vendor exploits them. A new technology that makes migration cheap, which AI-assisted data conversion may do, would weaken them.

Counter-positioning

This explains why strong incumbents so often fail to respond to a new model. Copying it would cut their existing profit, so each quarter the sensible choice is to wait. The dilemma of the licence vendor facing a subscription entrant, or the car maker facing direct online sales, has this shape.

Testing a claimed advantage

Ask two things of any "advantage" in a strategy document. Does it show up as better margins or returns than rivals over several years? And what specifically stops a well-funded competitor from doing the same? If the second answer is "nothing, but we are ahead", it is a lead and it will shrink.

In your job

When assessing an employer, apply the same test. A firm with real power can keep investing in people through downturns. A firm living on a lead that is being competed away will cut. This matters more to a ten-year career than the starting salary.

Advantages erode. Two bodies of work describe how industries are overturned and how firms can escape head-on competition.

Disruptive innovation

Clayton Christensen's theory is narrower than everyday use of the word. It describes a specific pattern.

  1. Incumbents improve their products faster than most customers need, serving their most demanding and profitable customers.
  2. An entrant offers something simpler and cheaper that is worse on the measures incumbents care about. It appeals to customers the incumbents overserve, or to people who were not customers at all.
  3. Incumbents ignore it, rationally: margins are lower and their best customers do not want it.
  4. The entrant improves. Once it is good enough for the mainstream, it takes the market with a lower cost structure.

You read a version of this in the Month 4 GMAT passage. Classic cases include steel mini-mills against integrated mills and small disk drives against large ones. The dilemma is that listening to your best customers and pursuing higher margins, which is normally good management, is what causes the failure.

Sustaining innovation

Most innovation is sustaining: it makes good products better for existing customers. Incumbents usually win those contests. A technology can be radical and still sustaining. The useful question about any new technology is which of the two patterns it follows in a given market.

Responding

  • Watch the low end and non-customers, as well as your key accounts.
  • Set up a separate unit with its own cost structure and measures to pursue the new model. Inside the main business it will be starved.
  • Accept that the new unit may take sales from the old.

Blue ocean strategy

Kim and Mauborgne argue that firms can create new market space by changing what an industry competes on. Their tool is a strategy canvas that lists the factors an industry competes on and asks four questions: which factors to eliminate, reduce, raise and create. The aim is a different value curve from competitors', raising value and lowering cost together.

Critics note that new space is soon contested, and that the examples were chosen after the fact. The four-question exercise is still a useful way to challenge an industry's habits.

What might follow these patterns near you

  • Lightweight, cloud-native engineering tools aimed at small manufacturers that big-platform vendors cannot serve profitably.
  • AI assistants that let a generalist engineer do tasks that needed a specialist.
  • Low-cost collaborative robots and simple fixtures replacing custom cells for low-volume work.

Whether any of these becomes good enough for the mainstream is uncertain. The framework tells you what evidence to watch: improvement rate against what mainstream customers need.

In your job

Apply the theory to skills. Tasks at the "overserved" end of your work, where a simpler tool is nearly good enough, are the ones automation will take first. Building judgement that such tools cannot yet supply is the personal equivalent of moving up-market with open eyes.

An incumbent's hardest strategic decision: fight, ignore, or change.

The situation

Meridian PLM Software earns ₹500 crore of revenue at a 25% operating margin. A venture-funded entrant, Loomwork, has launched a browser-based product at a fifth of Meridian's price. It handles basic data management and change workflows and lacks most of Meridian's depth in simulation, manufacturing planning and compliance.

In its first year Loomwork won 150 small manufacturers, none of them Meridian customers. This year two of Meridian's mid-sized customers moved a division onto it. Meridian's sales head calls it a toy. Its head of product is worried. Loomwork ships improvements every month.

Meridian's small-customer segment is 15% of revenue and its least profitable. The options being discussed are: ignore Loomwork; cut prices for small customers; launch a light product of its own; or acquire Loomwork.

Your questions

  1. Does Loomwork fit the pattern of disruptive innovation? What evidence would settle it?
  2. Why is ignoring it tempting, and what is the risk?
  3. Evaluate the price cut.
  4. What would a light product launched by Meridian need in order to succeed?
  5. What do you recommend?
Model answer

1. It fits the early stages: cheaper, simpler, worse on the measures Meridian's best customers value, winning non-customers first, and now touching the low end of Meridian's base. The deciding evidence is its rate of improvement against what mid-sized customers need. If the gap is closing, it is disruptive. If its architecture cannot support complex products and regulated processes, it will stay in a niche. The two divisions that moved are an early sign worth understanding in detail.

2. Tempting because the threatened segment is 15% of revenue and the least profitable, so abandoning it would raise Meridian's average margin. That is the trap Christensen describes: retreating up-market looks good each year until the entrant follows. The risk is losing the base on which Loomwork builds scale, data and references.

3. A price cut for small customers costs revenue on customers Meridian would have kept anyway, signals that list prices are negotiable, and does not address the reasons customers choose Loomwork: simplicity and fast deployment. Meridian's cost to serve small customers is also too high to match a fifth of the price profitably.

4. A separate team with its own budget, a cost structure built for low prices, self-service sales, and permission to take small customers from the main product. It must not be judged on Meridian's margin targets or sold by the enterprise sales force, who have no incentive. It needs a clear upgrade path into the full platform, which is the one thing Loomwork cannot offer.

5. Do not ignore it and do not cut prices. Start the separate light offering now, and evaluate an acquisition in parallel: buying Loomwork gives speed and a tested product, at a price that includes its investors' hopes, and it would need the same independence afterwards. Meanwhile strengthen what Loomwork cannot do, such as manufacturing depth and compliance, and make sure mid-sized customers actually use those capabilities, since unused depth is no defence.

What the case teaches. The right response to a low-end entrant usually feels wrong to the existing organisation, because it means competing with yourself at lower margins. That is why it needs a decision from the top and a separate structure.

Eight minutes.

It is widely believed that markets with network effects end with a single dominant firm. Because each additional user makes a network more valuable to the others, the argument runs, an early lead feeds on itself until rivals are starved of users. The reasoning is valid as far as it goes, but it rests on an assumption that often fails: that users belong to only one network at a time.

Where joining a second network is cheap, users commonly join several. A driver can accept rides through two applications; a merchant can accept several payment methods. This practice, known as multi-homing, weakens the self-reinforcing loop, because a rival need not persuade users to abandon the leader, only to add an alternative. Markets with extensive multi-homing tend to support several competitors and modest margins, however strong the underlying network effect.

The intensity of the effect matters as well. In some markets the hundredth participant adds as much value as the tenth; in others, value levels off once a user's immediate contacts or local suppliers are present. Local or quickly saturating network effects leave room for regional and specialist rivals. Predictions of inevitable monopoly should therefore be treated with caution until these two conditions have been examined.
